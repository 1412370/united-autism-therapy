Autism support for parents in Orange County usually comes from five places: your child's pediatrician, the Regional Center of Orange County (RCOC), your local school district, your health plan, and therapy providers. You do not have to contact all of them at once. Start with the one that fits your child's age and where you are right now, and add the others over time.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Your pediatrician is often the first stop for screening and referrals.
> - RCOC serves babies and toddlers through Early Start (birth to 36 months) and people 3 and older through Lanterman services.
> - From age 3, your school district can assess your child for special education and an IEP.
> - Your health plan, including Medi-Cal plans like CalOptima Health, can cover therapies such as ABA when medically necessary.
> - Parent support matters too. Orange County has free family resource centers staffed by parents.

## A quick map of autism resources in Orange County

Here is who does what, at a glance. Details and contact numbers follow.

| Where to turn | What they can help with | Ages |
|---|---|---|
| Pediatrician | Developmental screening, referrals for evaluation | All ages |
| Help Me Grow Orange County | Free help connecting to community services | Prenatal through 17 |
| RCOC Early Start | Evaluation and early intervention for delays or risk | Birth to 36 months |

## Step 1: Talk with your pediatrician

If you have noticed differences in how your child communicates, plays, or responds to others, bring it up at the next well-child visit, or call to schedule one sooner. You know your child best. Specific examples help.

Before the visit, jot down:

- What you have noticed, and when it started
- A few short phone videos of the behavior in everyday settings
- Any skills your child used to have and no longer uses
- Questions you want answered before you leave

Your pediatrician may do a developmental screening, refer you for a full evaluation, or both. Our guide on [when to get an autism evaluation](/blog/when-to-get-an-autism-evaluation/) explains the difference between screening and diagnosis and who can diagnose autism.

## Step 2: Get a developmental screening

A screening is a short check that shows whether a child might need a closer look. It is not a diagnosis.

In Orange County, RCOC notes that a free developmental screening is available for children ages 0 to 5 who live in the county, offered through Family Support Network at locations around Orange County. RCOC also points out that these screenings are not meant to replace doctor visits.

If you are not sure where to begin, [Help Me Grow Orange County](https://helpmegrow.choc.org/) is a free service run by CHOC that helps families connect to community services for child development. You can reach it at (866) 476-9025.

## Step 3: Contact the Regional Center of Orange County

The [Regional Center of Orange County](https://www.rcocdd.com/client-services/intake/) is a nonprofit contracted by the State of California to coordinate services and supports for people with developmental disabilities and their families. Its main number is 714-796-5100, and its office is at 1525 N Tustin Ave in Santa Ana.

RCOC works with families through different programs depending on age.

### Early Start: birth to 36 months

[Early Start](https://www.rcocdd.com/client-services/intake/early-start/) serves infants and toddlers who have a developmental delay or are at risk. RCOC lists three ways a child can qualify:

- A developmental delay of at least 25% in one or more areas, such as communication, social-emotional, cognitive, adaptive, or physical and motor skills
- An established risk condition with a high probability of delayed development
- A high risk of a substantial developmental disability due to a combination of biomedical risk factors

The child must also live in Orange County. Because eligibility is based on development and risk, you do not need to wait for an autism diagnosis to reach out. Learn more in our article on [early intervention for autism](/blog/early-intervention-for-autism/).

### Lanterman services: age 3 and older

For children 3 and older, RCOC provides [Lanterman services](https://www.rcocdd.com/client-services/intake/lanterman/). Autism is one of the qualifying developmental disabilities. RCOC says the disability must begin before age 18, be expected to continue indefinitely, and cause significant limitations in three or more areas of life, such as self-care, language, learning, or self-direction.

RCOC also notes that having a diagnosis is not, on its own, enough to qualify. Its intake team assesses each person's eligibility.

### How to start with RCOC

You can apply or ask questions through RCOC's online inquiry form or by phone. Physicians can also refer patients with RCOC's referral form, after which the family contacts the intake team. RCOC offers guides to the regional center system in several languages, including English, Spanish, and Vietnamese.

## Step 4: Connect with your school district

Once your child turns 3, your local public school district becomes an important partner. The [Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA)](https://sites.ed.gov/idea/) covers children and youth ages 3 through 21 under Part B and supports a free appropriate public education for eligible students with disabilities.

In practice, that means you can ask your school district to assess your child for special education. If your child qualifies, the team writes an Individualized Education Program (IEP) that lays out goals, services, and supports at school.

A few tips:

- **Ask in writing.** Send a dated email or letter to your district's special education office asking for an assessment. Keep a copy.
- **Find the right district.** School district boundaries may not match city lines, so neighbors in the same city can be in different districts. Check your home address with your district of residence before you call.
- **Bring reports.** Share evaluations, therapy progress notes, and your own observations.
- **Ask about transition.** If your child is in Early Start, ask RCOC and the district how the move to school-based services at age 3 will work.

## Step 5: Talk with your health plan

Your health plan can cover many therapies autistic children use. What is covered, and how, depends on your plan.

- **Medi-Cal:** Behavioral Health Treatment, including ABA, is a Medi-Cal benefit for members under 21 when a physician or psychologist determines it is medically necessary. Many Orange County Medi-Cal members are enrolled in CalOptima Health, which handles referrals and authorization for its members.
- **Commercial insurance:** California law requires health plans to cover behavioral health treatment for autism, but networks, paperwork, and costs vary.

Our guide to [Medi-Cal coverage for ABA therapy](/blog/does-medi-cal-cover-aba-therapy/) explains the steps and the questions to ask your plan.

## Step 6: Choose therapy providers

After an evaluation, your child's doctor or care team may recommend one or more therapies. The CDC describes several types of support for autistic children, including behavioral approaches like ABA and developmental approaches like speech and occupational therapy.

## Step 7: Take care of yourself, too

Parents often put their own needs last. But you will be making many decisions, and support makes that easier.

### Connect with other parents

RCOC's [Comfort Connection Family Resource Center](https://www.rcocdd.com/family-support/ccfrc/) connects families of children with special needs to resources and support, free of charge. It offers:

- Parent-to-parent support, including matching you with an experienced parent after a new diagnosis or during a big transition
- A lending library of books, articles, and videos in English, Spanish, and Vietnamese
- Emails about local community events

You can reach Comfort Connection at (714) 558-5400, Monday through Saturday, 8 AM to 6 PM

For help with school questions, [TASK (Team of Advocates for Special Kids)](https://www.taskca.org/) offers telephone support, IEP consultations, and workshops in English and Spanish for families of people from birth to age 26.

### Small habits that help

- **Keep one folder.** Store reports, plan letters, and IEP documents in one place, paper or digital.
- **Pick one next step a week.** You do not need to finish everything this month.
- **Share the load.** Let a partner, relative, or friend make a phone call or attend an appointment with you.
- **Protect some downtime.** A walk, a coffee with a friend, or ten quiet minutes still counts.

[Parent training](/services/parent-training/) can also help. When you have practical strategies for daily routines, like mealtime, bedtime, or leaving the park, home life often feels more manageable.
