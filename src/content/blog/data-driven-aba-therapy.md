ABA data collection is how your child's therapy team records what happens on each goal during sessions. Therapists note things like how often a skill happens, how long it lasts, or how much help your child needed. A Board Certified Behavior Analyst (BCBA) then reviews that information to decide what is working and what should change.

> **Key takeaways**
>
> - Data collection means recording your child's performance on specific goals during each session.
> - Common data types include frequency, duration, percentage correct, and prompt level.
> - Registered Behavior Technicians (RBTs) usually collect data; the BCBA reviews it and makes decisions.
> - Data is used to continue, change, or move past a goal, not to judge your child.
> - Your observations at home add context that numbers alone cannot show.

## What is ABA data collection?

Applied behavior analysis (ABA) relies on observation and measurement. The CDC notes that in ABA, [progress is tracked and measured](https://www.cdc.gov/autism/treatment/index.html). The National Institute of Child Health and Human Development describes ABA as [a widely accepted approach that tracks a child's progress in improving his or her skills](https://www.nichd.nih.gov/health/topics/autism/conditioninfo/treatments/behavioral-management).

In practice, this means each goal in your child's treatment plan has a clear way to measure it. If a goal is "asks for help using words, signs, or a picture," the therapist records each chance your child had to ask and what happened.

The value is not in collecting numbers for their own sake. Data should help the team make better decisions and help you see what is changing over time. If you want to understand how goals are written in the first place, see our guide to [ABA therapy goals](/blog/aba-therapy-goals/).

## What do ABA therapists track?

The type of data depends on the goal. Here are some common ones.

| Data type | What it measures | Example goal |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency | How many times something happens | Number of times your child asks for a snack using a picture card |
| Duration | How long something lasts | Minutes your child plays a game with a sibling |
| Latency | How long it takes to start after a direction | Seconds between "Shoes on" and starting to put on shoes |
| Percentage correct | Correct responses out of chances | Points to the right picture on 8 of 10 tries |
| Prompt level | How much help was needed | Washed hands with a gesture only, instead of hand-over-hand help |
| ABC data | What happened before and after a behavior | Notes on what came before and after a meltdown at bedtime |

A **prompt** is any help a therapist gives so your child can succeed, such as pointing, modeling, or guiding a hand. Tracking prompt level shows whether your child is becoming more independent.

**ABC data** stands for antecedent, behavior, and consequence: what happened right before, the behavior itself, and what happened right after. The CDC describes behavioral approaches as ones that [focus on changing behaviors by understanding what happens before and after the behavior](https://www.cdc.gov/autism/treatment/index.html). ABC notes help the BCBA understand *why* a hard behavior may be happening.

## Who collects the data, and who reviews it?

ABA is usually delivered by a team, and each person has a different role with data.

- **The RBT (Registered Behavior Technician)** often works with your child in sessions and records data on each goal as they teach. The BACB's RBT Ethics Code says RBTs [accurately complete all required documentation](https://www.bacb.com/ethics-information/ethics-codes/), including client data.
- **The BCBA** reviews the data, looks for patterns, and decides what to do next. The BACB describes the BCBA as a [graduate-level certification in behavior analysis](https://www.bacb.com/bcba/).
- **You** share what you see at home, at school, and in the community.

To learn more about how these two roles differ, read [BCBA vs RBT](/blog/bcba-vs-rbt/).

## How a BCBA uses data to adjust the plan

The BACB's [Ethics Code for Behavior Analysts](https://www.bacb.com/ethics-information/ethics-codes/) says behavior analysts graphically display, summarize, and use data to make decisions about continuing, modifying, or ending services. It also says that if data show desired outcomes are not being reached, behavior analysts should assess the situation and take corrective action.

In everyday terms, the BCBA looks at the graph for each goal and asks a few questions:

1. **Is the skill improving?** If yes, keep going and plan the next step.
2. **Has the skill leveled off?** The team may change the teaching method, the materials, or the reinforcer.
3. **Is the skill getting harder?** Something may have changed, like sleep, illness, or a new school routine. Your input helps here.
4. **Is the skill mastered?** The team checks that it holds up with different people and places, then moves to a new goal.

Here is an example. Your child is learning to ask for help. After a few weeks, the graph shows steady progress with a therapist but almost no asking with parents. The BCBA might add [parent training](/services/parent-training/) on that skill, or practice during real family routines.

## One goal from start to finish: a simple example

Here is how data might follow one goal over time. The numbers are only an illustration.

**The goal:** Your child puts on their shoes on their own before leaving the house.

1. **Baseline.** Before teaching starts, the RBT records what your child does now. Maybe your child needs full help with every step. This starting point is called a **baseline**, and it gives the team something to compare against.
2. **Teaching.** The BCBA breaks the skill into small steps: pick up the shoe, open the strap, slide the foot in, close the strap. The RBT teaches each step and notes how much help was needed.
3. **Review.** After a few weeks, the BCBA sees on the graph that your child does the first two steps alone but still needs help with the strap. The plan might switch to shoes that are easier to fasten, or add more practice on that one step.
4. **Checking it holds up.** Once your child does it alone with the RBT, the team checks that it also works with you, on busy school mornings.
5. **Mastery.** When the skill is steady, the goal is marked as mastered and practiced now and then to keep it strong.

At each stage, the data answers the same question: what does your child need next?

## How progress is shared with parents

Data is only useful if you can understand it. Many teams share progress through:

- **Quick check-ins** at the end of sessions, especially with [in-home ABA therapy](/services/in-home-aba/), where you can see the work happening
- **Parent meetings** with the BCBA to review graphs and talk about next steps
- **Written progress reports** that summarize each goal over a longer stretch of time

The BACB ethics code also says behavior analysts should involve families in [continual progress monitoring](https://www.bacb.com/ethics-information/ethics-codes/). Good updates use plain language: what your child can do now, what is still hard, and what comes next.

### Questions to ask about your child's data

- How are you measuring each of my child's goals?
- How often does the BCBA review the data?
- Can you show me a graph and explain what it means?
- What will you change if a goal is not moving?
- What should I watch for or share from home?

For a broader list of questions to ask providers, see [how to choose an ABA provider](/blog/how-to-choose-an-aba-provider/).

## What data cannot show

Numbers do not tell the whole story. A graph cannot show that your child laughed with a sibling for the first time, or that a skill seems to tire them out. A rough week can come from a cold, poor sleep, or a big change at home.

That is why your observations matter. A short note about a new word or a hard morning gives the BCBA context. Our guide on [supporting ABA therapy at home](/blog/supporting-aba-therapy-at-home/) includes tips for keeping a simple home log.

Data should also never replace attention to your child's comfort and willingness to take part. A good team watches both.

Progress varies for every child, and data helps the team respond to your child as an individual. Talk with your pediatrician or a qualified professional about your child's specific needs.
