Echolalia is repeating sounds, words, phrases or songs that someone else has said. It's common in toddlers, and it's common in autistic children who use speech. The American Speech-Language-Hearing Association (ASHA) says it was once seen as meaningless, but research has found that it often does real communication work.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Echolalia means repeating what others say. It can happen right away (immediate) or later (delayed).
> - Many toddlers echo as they learn language, and it usually fades around age 3.
> - It's also common in autistic children who use speech. Researchers describe purposes such as requesting, labeling, turn-taking and protesting.
> - You can respond to the message behind the echo, model a simple version and give your child time.
> - Check in with your pediatrician or a speech-language pathologist if echolalia continues past about 3, returns after clear communication, or comes with other delays or lost skills.

## What is echolalia?

ASHA defines echolalia as [repeating others' sounds, words, phrases or songs](https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/clinical-topics/autism/). The repeated words might come from a person, a video, a book or a commercial.

You might also hear the word "scripting." Some families use it for repeated lines from shows, books or conversations.

Echolalia isn't a diagnosis. It's a way of using language. The CDC lists [repeating words or phrases over and over](https://www.cdc.gov/autism/signs-symptoms/index.html) among the restricted or repetitive behaviors that can be part of autism, alongside movements such as flapping and rocking. Showing up on that list doesn't mean every child who echoes is autistic. Our guide to the [early signs of autism](/blog/early-signs-of-autism/) puts it in context with other signs.

## Immediate vs delayed echolalia

ASHA separates echolalia by timing. Immediate echolalia happens within seconds or minutes. Delayed echolalia happens after a long gap.

Here are two everyday, made-up examples:

- **Immediate:** You ask, "Do you want juice?" Your child says, "Want juice?" right back.
- **Delayed:** Your child says a line from a favorite show while pulling you toward the kitchen, hours after the show ended.

Cleveland Clinic adds another way to sort it. It describes "mitigated" echolalia, where the child changes the echo a bit, and "unmitigated" echolalia, where it's repeated exactly. Cleveland Clinic calls the behavior automatic, but ASHA, the Hanen Centre and a 2022 research review all describe communicative purposes. That's the view this article follows.

## Why toddlers echo, and why it usually fades

Echoing is part of how many young children learn to talk. ZERO TO THREE calls echolalia [a normal part of early language](https://www.zerotothree.org/resource/echolalia-definition/) that usually fades around age 3. Cleveland Clinic says the same thing: it's [common in toddlers and typically fades by about age 3](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/echolalia).

Say your toddler hears "all done" over and over at mealtimes and starts saying it too. That's practice. They're trying out the sounds and the rhythm before they can build a sentence of their own.

## Why autistic children use echolalia

ASHA says echolalia is [common in autistic people who use speech](https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/clinical-topics/autism/). The Hanen Centre explains that many autistic children learn language in [longer memorized chunks](https://www.hanen.org/information-tips/3-things-you-should-know-about-echolalia) instead of single words. A whole phrase can feel like one unit to the child.

Researchers describe several jobs that echoes can do. ASHA lists turn-taking, labeling, requesting, affirming and protesting. A 2022 review by [Cohn and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9620688/) adds agreeing, expressing emotion, rehearsing language and self-regulation. Here's how that might look in a hypothetical scene:

- At the grocery store, your child repeats a line from a cartoon about snacks. They may be asking for a snack.
- Your child echoes, "Time to go!" when you say it, then heads for the door. They may be saying yes.
- Your child recites a favorite song in a loud room. It may be helping them feel steady.

The Hanen Centre notes that echolalia may increase when a child is tired, confused or frustrated. So a sudden rise in echoing can be a clue that something is hard right now.

A note on the research. The Cohn review looked at six studies with between 1 and 14 participants each and was written mainly for educators. Its authors argue that trying to remove echolalia can backfire, and they found little evidence that functional language can be taught after echolalia is removed. That's one group's reading of a small body of work, so treat it as a useful perspective, not a final answer.

You may also see "gestalt language processing" online. ASHA says speech-language pathologists can apply that theory, but it cautions against overgeneralizing it to autistic language development. It isn't an established explanation for every autistic child.

## How echolalia can change over time

The Hanen Centre describes a common path. A child may start with whole memorized chunks, then change parts of a phrase, then recombine pieces into new phrases and more flexible speech. Not every child follows this path, and it can take a long time. But a changed echo is worth noticing. It may be a sign your child is moving toward more self-generated language, as the Cohn authors suggest.

## What parents can do

There's no single script, and you don't need to be perfect. These ideas come from the guidance in ZERO TO THREE and the Hanen Centre.

1. **Note the context.** What happened right before? Where were you? What did your child want, feel or see? The same phrase can mean different things in different moments.
2. **Respond to the message.** If the echo sounds like a request, answer the request. "You want a snack. Here you go."
3. **Model a simple version.** Offer a short phrase your child could try, such as "Snack, please." Say it once, then wait.
4. **Give extra time.** ZERO TO THREE suggests giving your child time to respond. A slow breath before you fill the silence can help.
5. **Don't treat it as meaningless.** Even when you can't figure out the message, staying warm and interested tells your child that talking with you works.
6. **Try breaking a chunk into parts.** The Hanen Centre suggests helping children break memorized phrases into smaller pieces over time. An SLP can show you how for your child's favorite phrases.

Some children also benefit from other ways to communicate while speech develops. Our article on [AAC and autism](/blog/aac-and-autism/) explains options such as pictures and devices. And if your child uses echoes to say "no" or "I need help," our guide to [functional communication training](/blog/functional-communication-training-aba/) shows how teams can build clearer ways to say it.

## When to talk to a pediatrician or speech-language pathologist

ZERO TO THREE and Cleveland Clinic point to similar times to check in:

- Echolalia continues past about age 3.
- It returns after a period when your child was communicating clearly.
- It shows up with trouble using language, other delays, or a loss of skills (ZERO TO THREE also lists this).

If any of these fit, bring it up with your pediatrician. ZERO TO THREE says next steps may include developmental screening, a speech-language evaluation or an early intervention referral. You don't have to wait for the next well-child visit to ask. Our guide on [autism treatment options](/blog/autism-treatment-options/) gives a wider view of the kinds of support families may explore.

## What a speech-language pathologist looks at

A speech-language pathologist (SLP) is a specialist in communication. ASHA says SLPs assess all expressive communication, including echolalia, and look at [function and intent](https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/clinical-topics/autism/). They also use language sampling, which means recording and studying how your child actually talks.

In practice, an SLP may ask when the echoes happen, what your child seems to want or feel, and which parts change from one use to the next. From there, goals are usually built around your child's strengths and your family's priorities. ASHA describes care that supports a child's identity while building skills, and does not aim to force a child to hide how they communicate.
