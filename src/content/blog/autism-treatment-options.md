Autism treatment options for children include behavioral approaches such as applied behavior analysis (ABA), speech-language therapy, occupational therapy, developmental and parent-mediated approaches, and early intervention for babies and toddlers. There is no single best treatment for every child. Most families use a mix of supports, chosen around their child's needs, strengths and daily life.

> **Key takeaways**
> - There is no one standard treatment for autism. The right mix is different for each child.
> - The CDC says behavioral approaches, including ABA, have the most evidence.
> - Speech-language therapy and occupational therapy are common developmental approaches.
> - Children under 3 can get early intervention, and a doctor's referral is not needed to ask.
> - Medication does not treat autism itself. It can help with some co-occurring symptoms.
> - Be cautious of products sold with big promises. The FDA warns about unapproved autism products.

## Is there one best treatment for autism?

No. The National Institute of Child Health and Human Development (NICHD) says there is [no single standard treatment for autism](https://www.nichd.nih.gov/health/topics/autism/conditioninfo/treatments), and that the best approach differs from person to person. The National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) also says there is [no single best approach](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/autism-spectrum-disorder). It adds that programs are often structured and intensive, and may involve family members.

In this guide, "treatment" means support that helps your child build skills, communicate and take part in daily life. It does not mean changing who your child is. The CDC explains that treatment aims to [reduce symptoms that get in the way of daily life](https://www.cdc.gov/autism/treatment/index.html), and that it can happen at school, at home, or in health care and community settings.

## Types of autism therapy at a glance

The CDC groups autism treatments into a few main types. Many children use more than one at the same time.

| Type | What it focuses on | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Behavioral | Teaching useful skills and reducing behaviors that get in the way | ABA, including discrete trial training and pivotal response training |
| Developmental | Specific skills such as language and daily living | Speech-language therapy, occupational therapy, physical therapy |
| Naturalistic developmental behavioral | Learning through play and routines, following the child's lead | Early Start Denver Model, JASPER, Project ImPACT |
| Educational | Learning in classroom settings | TEACCH |
| Social-relational | Social connection and relationships | DIR/Floortime, Social Stories, social skills groups |
| Psychological | Coping with anxiety or depression | Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) |
| Medication | Co-occurring symptoms, not autism itself | Prescribed and monitored by a doctor |

Sources: [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/autism/treatment/index.html) and [Schreibman and colleagues, 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4513196/).

## Behavioral approaches, including ABA

Applied behavior analysis (ABA) is an approach that teaches skills [mainly through positive reinforcement](https://www.bacb.com/about-behavior-analysis/), according to the Behavior Analyst Certification Board (BACB). Positive reinforcement means following a behavior with something your child values, so the behavior is more likely to happen again.

The CDC says behavioral approaches have [the most evidence for treating symptoms of autism](https://www.cdc.gov/autism/treatment/index.html) and names ABA as the main one. ABA can be taught in different styles. NICHD's page on [behavioral management therapy](https://www.nichd.nih.gov/health/topics/autism/conditioninfo/treatments/behavioral-management) describes several, including discrete trial teaching, which uses short, structured teaching steps, and pivotal response training, a more play-based style covered in the developmental section below. NICHD also describes early intensive behavioral intervention for very young children, which is individualized and asks a lot of a family's time.

A Board Certified Behavior Analyst (BCBA) designs and supervises an ABA program, and a Registered Behavior Technician (RBT) often works with your child in sessions. Our guide to [BCBA vs RBT](/blog/bcba-vs-rbt/) explains who does what. If ABA is new to you, start with [what ABA therapy is](/blog/what-is-aba-therapy/).

ABA can happen in a center, at school or at home. [In-home ABA therapy](/services/in-home-aba/) lets your child practice skills in the routines where they matter most, such as meals, play and getting ready for bed.

## Speech-language therapy

The CDC calls speech and language therapy the [most common developmental therapy](https://www.cdc.gov/autism/treatment/index.html) for autistic children. A speech-language pathologist (SLP) is a specialist in communication, including understanding, expression and social communication.

According to the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association (ASHA), SLPs [assess language and social communication](https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/clinical-topics/autism/), set social-language goals, check whether augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) would help, and train children and families to use it. ASHA describes care that is family-centered and neurodiversity-affirming, built around a person's goals, strengths and identity.

AAC means any way of communicating besides speech, from gestures and pictures to speech-generating devices and apps. An ASHA-affiliated consumer site notes that AAC [has no prerequisites](https://helpingyoucommunicate.org/article/augmentative-and-alternative-communication-aac): children do not need to reach certain milestones first, and children under 3 can use it.

Learn more in our guide to [speech therapy for autism](/blog/speech-therapy-for-autism/), or see our [speech and language therapy](/services/speech-language-therapy/) service.

## Occupational therapy

Occupational therapy (OT) helps children take part in everyday activities. The CDC says OT [builds daily living skills](https://www.cdc.gov/autism/treatment/index.html) and can include sensory integration therapy. Sensory integration therapy focuses on how a child takes in and responds to sights, sounds, textures and movement.

For a young child, OT goals might include using a spoon, getting an arm into a sleeve, or tolerating new textures. Our article on [occupational therapy for autism](/blog/occupational-therapy-for-autism/) has more examples, and our [occupational therapy](/services/occupational-therapy/) page explains how we work with families.

## Developmental and parent-mediated approaches

Naturalistic developmental behavioral interventions (NDBIs) blend ABA with what is known about how young children develop. In a 2015 review, Schreibman and colleagues describe NDBIs as teaching [in natural settings and play](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4513196/), with shared control, the child's own interests and natural rewards. A natural reward is the thing the child was asking for, such as getting the ball after reaching for it or saying "ball."

Common NDBI targets are joint attention (sharing focus on something with another person), imitation, play and early language. Examples include pivotal response training, the Early Start Denver Model, JASPER and Project ImPACT, and the authors treat NDBIs as a form of ABA. The CDC notes that one of them, the Early Start Denver Model, applies ABA principles to [children aged 12 to 48 months](https://www.cdc.gov/autism/treatment/index.html) through play and shared attention.

Parent-mediated therapy means a professional trains you to use therapy strategies with your own child, so your child gets practice all through the day. NICHD says [studies suggest gains in communication and interaction](https://www.nichd.nih.gov/health/topics/autism/conditioninfo/treatments/parent-mediated), and that the evidence is still developing. [Parent training](/services/parent-training/) is one way families build these skills.

## Early intervention for babies and toddlers

Early intervention is support for young children with developmental delays or disabilities. The CDC explains that early intervention [serves children from birth to 3](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/early-intervention/index.html) through state programs and is free or low-cost. Eligibility is based on an evaluation, and the CDC says "a doctor's referral is not necessary." Children 3 and older can be evaluated through their local public school.

You also do not have to wait for a diagnosis. NICHD notes that recent guidelines recommend starting developmental and behavioral intervention [as soon as autism is diagnosed or seriously suspected](https://www.nichd.nih.gov/health/topics/autism/conditioninfo/treatments/early-intervention).

## What medication can and cannot do

Medication does not treat autism itself. NIMH says providers may prescribe medication [for specific symptoms, not autism itself](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/autism-spectrum-disorder), such as aggression, anxiety, attention problems, hyperactivity, irritability, and repetitive or self-injurious behavior.

Some autistic children also have co-occurring conditions, which are conditions that happen alongside autism. The CDC notes that medication may help with co-occurring issues such as [seizures, sleep problems and gastrointestinal problems](https://www.cdc.gov/autism/treatment/index.html). It advises working with an experienced doctor and watching for side effects.

NICHD says medication [works best when combined with behavioral therapy](https://www.nichd.nih.gov/health/topics/autism/conditioninfo/treatments/medication-treatment), and that all medications carry risks. It lists risperidone (for ages 5 to 16) and aripiprazole as FDA-approved for irritability associated with autism.

Your pediatrician or a qualified specialist is the right person to talk with about whether medication makes sense for your child.

## How to spot risky or unproven treatments

The CDC lists complementary and alternative approaches, such as special diets, supplements, chiropractic care, and animal or arts therapies. It advises [talking with a doctor before starting any of them](https://www.cdc.gov/autism/treatment/index.html).

Some products go further and are sold as autism treatments without FDA approval. The FDA calls these products ["misleading and deceptive"](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/medication-health-fraud/medication-health-fraud-specific-diseases-and-conditions) and warns that they can cause serious health problems and give families false hope. One example is over-the-counter chelation products, which have been marketed to people with autism. The FDA has [never approved any chelation product for over-the-counter use](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/medication-health-fraud/questions-and-answers-unapproved-chelation-products), and chelation can cause dehydration, kidney failure and death.

These warning signs can help you pause and ask questions:

- It promises to make autism go away, or says it works for every child.
- It claims fast, dramatic results.
- It relies on personal stories instead of research.
- It pressures you to buy now or pay a large amount up front.
- It tells you to stop other therapies or skip your child's doctor.
- It says something you swallow, inject or rub on is "natural," so it has no side effects.

If you are unsure, bring the product or website to your pediatrician before trying it.

## How to build a treatment plan for your child

Because no single answer fits every child, a good plan is about fit. These steps can help:

1. **Start with daily life.** Write down what is hardest right now, such as communication, sleep, safety or routines, and what your child enjoys and does well.
2. **Talk with your pediatrician** about which evaluations and referrals make sense.
3. **Choose a setting.** Support can happen at home, at school, or in a clinic or community setting.
4. **Ask providers good questions** about credentials, how goals are chosen with your family, how progress is measured, and how your child's comfort and assent are respected. Assent means paying attention to whether your child is willing to take part. Our checklist on [how to choose an ABA provider](/blog/how-to-choose-an-aba-provider/) covers this in detail.
5. **Check coverage.** Many plans, including Medi-Cal, cover ABA therapy for children with an autism diagnosis. Our [insurance page](/insurance/) explains how we help families with coverage and authorization.
6. **Review progress together.** Plans should change as your child grows and as your family's priorities shift.

Every child is different, so talk with your pediatrician or a qualified professional about your child's specific needs.
