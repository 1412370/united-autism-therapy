Stimming means repetitive movements or sounds, such as hand flapping, rocking, spinning, pacing or repeating a phrase. The American Psychiatric Association (APA) describes it as [repetitive behavior with a purpose](https://psychiatry.org/news-room/apa-blogs/understand-stimming-repetitive-behaviors-purpose). For most children, the usual advice is to leave harmless stims alone and look for support only when a stim causes harm or gets in the way of learning.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Stimming is repetitive movement or sound. It's common in autistic children, and it also happens in people who aren't autistic.
> - Autistic adults describe stimming mostly as a way to calm down, manage feelings and focus.
> - The NHS advises not to change an autistic child's behavior unless it harms the child or others.
> - A stim may need support if it causes injury, such as head banging, or blocks learning.
> - Safer swaps like fidget items can help when a stim hurts. Talk with your pediatrician about stims that cause harm.

## What is stimming?

Stimming is an informal word for a group of repetitive movements and sounds. The APA says it can involve the body or objects, and it includes things like [hand flapping, rocking, pacing or repeating phrases](https://psychiatry.org/news-room/apa-blogs/understand-stimming-repetitive-behaviors-purpose). The Transmitter, a news site covering autism research, calls it an informal term for [a subset of repetitive movements and sounds](https://thetransmitter.org/spectrum/repetitive-behaviors-and-stimming-in-autism-explained).

The word doesn't appear on every official page. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) doesn't use "stimming," but it does list [hand flapping, body rocking and spinning](https://www.cdc.gov/autism/signs-symptoms/index.html) among the repetitive behaviors that can be part of autism. Same behaviors, different label.

## What does stimming look like?

Stims come in many forms, and they're different from child to child. Common examples include:

- Flapping or shaking the hands
- Rocking the body back and forth
- Spinning in circles, or spinning an object
- Pacing
- Repeating words, sounds or phrases
- Tapping, clicking or rubbing an object

Say your child flaps their hands every time their favorite show comes on. Or they hum the same three notes while they wait in line. Neither of those is a problem to solve. They're things a child does with their body or voice, and the APA says they can serve many purposes, from calming down to expressing frustration.

Repeating phrases counts as a stim too, according to the APA.

## Do only autistic people stim?

No. The APA says stimming is [most common in autistic children and children with intellectual disability](https://psychiatry.org/news-room/apa-blogs/understand-stimming-repetitive-behaviors-purpose), and that it also happens in typically developing people. Everyday habits such as bouncing a leg may look similar. Plenty of adults do this without thinking about it.

So a single repeated movement doesn't tell you much. Stimming is only one piece of the picture. If you're worried about how your child is developing, talk with your pediatrician. Our guide to [early signs of autism](/blog/early-signs-of-autism/) lists other things to watch for.

## Why do children stim?

The APA says stimming can help with calming, sensory input, sensory overload, and expressing frustration or discomfort. Sensory input means the information your body takes in from touch, sound, sight and movement. Our guide to [sensory processing and autism](/blog/sensory-processing-and-autism/) goes deeper on that side of things.

Some of the clearest answers come from autistic people themselves. A 2019 study by Kapp and colleagues, published in the journal Autism, asked autistic adults in the United Kingdom about stimming. The adults mainly described it as [a way to regulate themselves](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6728747): calming overwhelm, managing emotions and concentrating. They said the hard part was usually how other people reacted, not the stimming itself. Many said they hid or swapped their stims in public, and that holding back took effort.

That study has limits. It was a small qualitative study of adults, so it doesn't tell us about young children, people who don't use speech, or people with significant intellectual disability. Still, it gives parents a useful way to think about it. Your child's stim may be doing a job.

The Transmitter adds a note of caution: researchers still have [little concrete evidence](https://thetransmitter.org/spectrum/repetitive-behaviors-and-stimming-in-autism-explained) about all the functions stimming serves. So "why" can be a guess. Watching when it happens is a good place to start.

## Should I stop my child from stimming?

For harmless stims, the general advice is no. The NHS in the UK says the usual approach is [not to try to change an autistic child's behavior](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/autism/autism-and-everyday-life/help-with-behaviour/) unless it harms the child or others. It describes stimming as repetitive movement that autistic children may find calming and enjoyable.

The American Speech-Language-Hearing Association (ASHA) takes a similar view. It describes neurodiversity-affirming care as supporting a person's identity while building skills, and it says the goal isn't to [force people to mask autistic traits](https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/clinical-topics/autism/). ASHA also lists reducing or hiding stimming hand movements as an example of camouflaging, which means hiding autistic traits to fit in.

Asking a child to hide a stim isn't a neutral thing to ask. Kapp's participants described holding back as tiring, and that was in adults. Keep that in mind before you decide a harmless stim needs to change.

## When might stimming need support?

Not every stim is harmless. The APA notes that some stimming can cause self-harm, such as head banging, and that it may not be possible or appropriate to stop it. The Transmitter says intervention is generally considered when a behavior [causes physical harm or interferes with learning](https://thetransmitter.org/spectrum/repetitive-behaviors-and-stimming-in-autism-explained) or other activities. Kapp's authors reach a similar place: harmful stims are unwanted, and intervention may be appropriate for them.

ASHA frames it around the person. It lists patterns that may need services as those that restrict opportunities, cause distress or affect self-management. These questions can help you decide:

- Is the stim hurting your child or someone else?
- Does it keep your child from joining in at school, at meals or at play?
- Does it seem to come from distress or being overwhelmed?

If you answer yes to any of these, tell your pediatrician, who can help you work out next steps. The NHS also points out that distress and overwhelm can lead to meltdowns or self-injury. Our article on [autism meltdowns vs. tantrums](/blog/autism-meltdown-vs-tantrum/) can help you read what's going on in the moment.

## What can you offer instead of a stim that hurts?

When a stim causes harm, the goal isn't to take something away. It's to meet the same need in a safer way. The APA suggests alternatives such as fidget toys. Start by watching what the stim seems to do for your child, then talk with your pediatrician or an OT about options that give the same kind of input more safely. The NHS also suggests changes to the environment, such as adjusting light and noise, keeping routines steady and warning your child early about changes. An occupational therapist (OT) can look at your child's sensory needs and help you find options that fit. ## What should you ask a provider about stimming?

If your child works with a therapist, it's okay to ask directly. Questions worth bringing up:

- How do you decide whether a stim needs support?
- Do you ever work to stop harmless stims? Why or why not?
- How do you find out what the stim does for my child?
- How will you involve my child and respect their comfort?
- What would you offer in place of a stim that causes injury?
