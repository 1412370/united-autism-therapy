An IEP, or Individualized Education Program, is a written plan that describes the special education and support services your child will receive at school. Public schools provide IEPs to eligible children from age 3 through 21 under the federal [Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA)](https://sites.ed.gov/idea/). For many autistic children in Orange County, the IEP is the main way to get support during the school day. This guide walks you through each step, from your first request to the meeting itself.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Start by sending your school district a **written** request for a special education assessment.
> - California law sets timelines: an assessment plan within about 15 days, and an IEP meeting within 60 days of your consent (not counting long breaks).
> - You are an equal member of the IEP team. You can ask questions, bring support, and take time before signing.
> - If your child is in Early Start, transition planning for age 3 should begin months before the third birthday.
> - Free help is available in Orange County, including family advocacy organizations.

*This guide is general information, not legal advice. Rules and timelines can change, so confirm details with your school district or a qualified advocate.*

## What is an IEP, and who qualifies?

An IEP is both a **meeting** and a **document**. The meeting brings together you, teachers, a district representative, and specialists. The document records what the team agrees on, including:

- **Present levels:** how your child is doing now, in learning, communication, behavior, and daily skills
- **Annual goals:** specific skills your child will work on over the next year
- **Services:** special education instruction plus related services, such as speech, occupational therapy, or behavior support
- **Accommodations:** changes that help your child access learning, like visual schedules or breaks
- **Placement:** where your child will learn, in the least restrictive environment that meets their needs

Autism is one of the disability categories under IDEA. To qualify, the school's assessment must show that your child has a disability **and** needs special education to make progress in school. A medical diagnosis can help, but the school makes its own decision.

## Step 1: Send a written request for assessment

Write a short, dated letter or email to your school's principal or your district's special education office. Say that you are requesting a **special education assessment** for your child and briefly explain your concerns.

**Simple example:**

> "I am requesting a special education assessment for my child, [Name], date of birth [date], in all areas of suspected disability. I am concerned about [communication, social interaction, behavior, learning]. Please send me an assessment plan."

Keep a copy. The date of your written request starts the clock.

## Step 2: Review and sign the assessment plan

Under California Education Code section 56043, the district generally has **15 days** (not counting school breaks longer than five days) to send you an **assessment plan**. This lists which tests and observations the school wants to do, such as speech-language, psychoeducational, or occupational therapy assessments.

You have **at least 15 days** to review it. Before you sign:

- Check that every area you are worried about is included.
- Ask in writing for an area to be added if something is missing.
- Ask who will do each assessment and how you will get the results.

## Step 3: The assessments

Once you sign, the school assesses your child. Most families are asked to fill in questionnaires too.

It helps to gather:

- Reports from your pediatrician, developmental specialists, or regional center
- Progress reports from your child's ABA, speech, or OT providers
- Notes on what works at home, such as visual schedules or calming strategies

Ask for copies of the assessment reports **before** the meeting, so you have time to read them.

## Step 4: The IEP meeting

The IEP meeting must be held within **60 days** of the district receiving your signed consent, again not counting school breaks longer than five days.

**How to prepare:**

- Write down your child's strengths. A good IEP starts with what your child does well.
- List your top three priorities for the year, like communicating needs, joining group activities, or handling transitions.
- Bring questions. For example: "How will we measure this goal?" or "How often will I get progress updates?"
- Bring support if you want. You can invite a family member, a friend, an advocate, or someone from your child's therapy team.

**During the meeting:**

- Ask the team to explain any term you don't understand.
- Make sure goals are specific and measurable. "Will use words, signs, or a device to ask for a break in 4 out of 5 opportunities" is clearer than "will improve communication."
- You don't have to sign at the meeting. You can take the IEP home to review.

## What kinds of goals and supports do autistic children often have?

Every IEP is individual, but common areas include:

| Area | Example support |
|---|---|
| Communication | Speech-language services, AAC devices, picture communication |
| Social skills | Structured peer activities, social narratives, small-group practice |
| Behavior and regulation | Visual schedules, planned breaks, a behavior intervention plan |
| Sensory needs | Occupational therapy, a quieter workspace, movement breaks |
| Daily living | Toileting, dressing, or lunchtime routines at school |
| Academics | Specialized instruction, modified assignments |

If your child has behaviors that get in the way of learning, ask whether a **functional behavior assessment (FBA)** is appropriate. An FBA looks at why a behavior happens, so the team can plan supports that address the cause. Read more about [how ABA goals are set](/blog/aba-therapy-goals/).

## IEP vs. 504 plan: what's the difference?

- An **IEP** provides specialized instruction and related services for students who qualify for special education under IDEA.
- A **504 plan**, under Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act, provides accommodations for students with disabilities who don't need specialized instruction. Examples include extra time, preferential seating, or a quiet testing space.

If the school suggests a 504 plan instead of an IEP, ask them to explain why. If you disagree, you can ask for that in writing.

## Moving from Early Start to an IEP at age 3

In California, [Early Start](https://www.dds.ca.gov/services/early-start/) serves babies and toddlers from birth to 36 months. In Orange County, Early Start is coordinated by the [Regional Center of Orange County (RCOC)](https://www.rcocdd.com/client-services/intake/early-start/). When your child turns 3, Early Start ends.

Under IDEA, a **transition conference** should take place at least **90 days** before your child's third birthday. Around then, your service coordinator and the school district plan:

- A school district assessment to see whether your child qualifies for preschool special education
- An IEP meeting, so services can start by the third birthday if your child qualifies
- Whether your child may qualify for [RCOC Lanterman services](https://www.rcocdd.com/client-services/intake/lanterman/) for ages 3 and older

**Tip:** Ask your service coordinator about transition planning around your child's second birthday, so there is plenty of time. See our guide to [early intervention for autism](/blog/early-intervention-for-autism/) for more on Early Start.

## What if I disagree with the school?

Disagreements happen. Try these steps in order:

1. **Ask questions in writing.** Request the reasoning behind a decision.
2. **Ask for another meeting.** You can request an IEP meeting to revisit goals or services.
3. **Get support.** In Orange County, [TASK (Team of Advocates for Special Kids)](https://www.taskca.org/) offers parent support, IEP consultations, and workshops.
4. **Know your formal options.** IDEA gives parents procedural safeguards, including mediation and due process. Your district must give you a copy of your rights.

## How therapy at home and school fit together

School services focus on what your child needs to learn and take part in school. Services like ABA through your health plan often focus on skills across home and community. The two work best when they are connected.

- Share your child's ABA progress reports with the IEP team, with your consent.
- Ask whether strategies that work at home, like a visual schedule or a "first, then" board, can be used at school too.
- Ask your BCBA what goals they suggest for school.
