Early intervention for autism means support that starts in the baby, toddler, and preschool years, such as parent coaching, speech therapy, occupational therapy, and developmental or behavioral therapy. Research suggests that starting early can make a real difference in how children build skills. In California, children from birth to age 3 can get early intervention through Early Start, and you do not need an autism diagnosis to ask for an evaluation.

> **Key takeaways**
> - The NICHD says early diagnosis and intervention, such as during preschool or before, are more likely to have major positive effects on symptoms and later skills.
> - Early intervention can include parent-mediated therapy, speech, occupational therapy, and developmental and behavioral approaches.
> - California Early Start serves infants and toddlers from birth to 3 with developmental delays or risk.
> - Anyone can refer a child to Early Start, and evaluation and service coordination are free.
> - At age 3, children move from Early Start to other services, often through their school district.

## Why early intervention matters

The early years are a time of fast learning. Children are building the foundations of communication, play, and daily routines. Support during this window can help your child practice those skills in everyday moments with the people they love.

The National Institute of Child Health and Human Development (NICHD) puts it this way on its [autism treatments page](https://www.nichd.nih.gov/health/topics/autism/conditioninfo/treatments): research shows that early diagnosis and interventions, such as during preschool or before, are more likely to have major positive effects on symptoms and later skills.

Two cautions matter here:

- **Results vary.** Every child is different, and early intervention is not a promise of any specific outcome.
- **It is never "too late."** Children keep learning and growing at every age. Starting early is helpful, but starting support later is still worthwhile.

The NICHD also notes that there is no one standard treatment for autism, and that the most effective therapies are often different for each person. Good early intervention is built around your child and your family.

## What early intervention for autism can include

Early intervention is not a single program. It is a mix of supports matched to your child's needs.

### Parent-mediated therapy

The NICHD lists parent-mediated therapy among autism treatments. In this approach, a therapist coaches you to use specific strategies during daily routines like bath time, meals, and play. Your child learns during the moments that happen every day, not only during therapy hours.

For example, a therapist might show you how to pause before handing your toddler a snack so they have a chance to reach, point, sign, or say a word to ask for it. [Parent training](/services/parent-training/) builds these skills over time.

### Speech and language therapy

Speech-language pathologists help children build communication in many forms: gestures, pictures, signs, devices, and spoken words. Early goals might include taking turns, responding to their name, or requesting a favorite toy. Our article on [speech therapy for autism](/blog/speech-therapy-for-autism/) goes deeper.

### Occupational therapy

Occupational therapists support fine motor skills, self-care like feeding and dressing, play, and sensory needs. For a toddler, this might look like practicing using a spoon, tolerating new textures, or building play skills with stacking toys.

### Developmental and behavioral approaches

The [CDC describes](https://www.cdc.gov/autism/treatment/index.html) behavioral approaches, including Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA), and developmental approaches, including speech and occupational therapy. For young children, ABA is often play-based and focused on meaningful goals such as communication, joining in play, and following simple routines. A BCBA designs the program, and it should respect your child's assent, comfort, and dignity.

If you are wondering when ABA can begin, our guide on [what age to start ABA therapy](/blog/what-age-to-start-aba-therapy/) covers that question.

### Other Early Start services

California's Department of Developmental Services (DDS) lists a wide range of [Early Start services](https://www.dds.ca.gov/services/early-start/what-is-early-start/), including:

- Family training and counseling
- Speech and language services
- Occupational and physical therapy
- Special instruction
- Psychological services
- Service coordination
- Assistive technology, audiology, and vision services

Not every child receives every service. Your child's plan is built around their needs.

## What early intervention can look like day to day

For a toddler, early intervention usually does not look like school. Much of it happens on the floor, at the table, or at the park, and it is built into routines your family already has.

Here is an example of how a few goals might fit into an ordinary morning:

| Routine | A possible goal | What it might look like |
|---|---|---|
| Breakfast | Requesting | Your child reaches, points, signs, or says "more" to get another bite |
| Getting dressed | Following simple steps | Your child pushes an arm through a sleeve with a little help |
| Play time | Shared attention | You and your child take turns rolling a ball and look at each other |
| Leaving the house | Handling transitions | A picture or a short song signals it is time to go |

A therapist or BCBA helps choose goals like these based on your child's needs and your family's priorities, then coaches you to practice them. Small, frequent chances to practice during the day often add up.

Good early intervention also watches for your child's signals. If your child seems upset, tired, or unwilling, a skilled therapist adjusts rather than pushing through. Your child's comfort and trust come first.

## California Early Start: early intervention from birth to 3

[Early Start](https://www.dds.ca.gov/services/early-start/) is California's early intervention program for infants and toddlers with developmental delays or at risk for a developmental disability. It is established under Part C of the federal [Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA)](https://sites.ed.gov/idea/) and California's Early Intervention Services Act. DDS oversees the program, and local regional centers deliver it.

### Who qualifies

DDS and the Regional Center of Orange County describe three ways a child under 3 can qualify:

1. **A developmental delay** of at least 25% in one or more areas: cognitive, communication, social or emotional, adaptive, or physical and motor development
2. **An established risk condition** of known cause with a high probability of delayed development
3. **A high risk** of a substantial developmental disability due to a combination of biomedical risk factors

### You do not need an autism diagnosis

Because eligibility is based on your child's development and risk, you do not need a diagnosis to make a referral. DDS says [anyone can make a referral](https://www.dds.ca.gov/services/early-start/what-is-early-start/), including parents, medical care providers, family members, foster parents, and day care providers.

This matters because a full autism evaluation can take time to schedule. You can request an Early Start evaluation while you wait. For more on the diagnostic side, see our guide on [when to get an autism evaluation](/blog/when-to-get-an-autism-evaluation/).

### What happens after a referral

According to DDS, within 45 days the regional center or local education agency completes evaluation and assessment. If your child is eligible, the team develops an Individualized Family Service Plan (IFSP) with you. The IFSP addresses your child's strengths and needs, your concerns as a parent, and the early intervention services your child will receive.

DDS notes that services are provided in the family home or other community settings, so support often happens where your child already spends time.

### What it costs

DDS states that there is no cost for evaluation, assessment, and service coordination. Health insurance may be used for some therapy services, and services not covered by insurance may be purchased by the regional center or local education agency. Ask your service coordinator how this applies to your family.

### How to start Early Start in Orange County

- **Statewide:** Call the Early Start BabyLine at 800-515-2229 (800-515-BABY).
- **Orange County:** Contact the [Regional Center of Orange County Early Start program](https://www.rcocdd.com/client-services/intake/early-start/) at 714-796-5100 or through its online inquiry form. Your child must live in Orange County.

When you call, have ready your child's name and birth date, your contact information, and a short description of your concerns.

## The transition at age 3

Early Start ends when your child turns 3. DDS describes this as a transition from Early Start to other services at age three, and it is worth planning ahead.

Common next steps include:

- **School district services.** IDEA Part B covers children and youth ages 3 through 21. Your school district can assess your child for special education and, if eligible, develop an Individualized Education Program (IEP).
- **Regional center services for ages 3 and up.** RCOC provides Lanterman services for people 3 and older with a qualifying developmental disability, including autism, and a provisional Lanterman category for ages 3 to 4.
- **Health plan–covered therapies.** Therapies such as ABA, speech, and OT may continue through your health plan when medically necessary.

Ask your service coordinator about transition planning well before your child's third birthday so there is time to schedule assessments and meetings.

Please talk with your pediatrician or a qualified professional about which early supports fit your child.
