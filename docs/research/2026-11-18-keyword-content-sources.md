## Topic 1: NEW article "Autism treatment options for children"

### Verified sources

**1.1 CDC, "Treatment and Intervention for Autism Spectrum Disorder"**
- URL: https://www.cdc.gov/autism/treatment/index.html · Publisher: CDC (NCBDDD) · Date shown: May 16, 2024 · Status: 200 · **verified (fetched)**
- Supports: Treatment aims to reduce symptoms that interfere with daily life. It can be delivered at school, in health or community settings, or at home.
- Supports: Behavioral approaches have the most evidence. ABA is the main one, and the page names discrete trial training and pivotal response training as ABA styles.
- Supports: Developmental approaches include speech-language therapy (the most common one), occupational therapy (daily living skills, can include sensory integration) and physical therapy. The Early Start Denver Model applies ABA principles to children aged 12 to 48 months through play.
- Supports: Educational (TEACCH) and social-relational approaches (DIR/Floortime, RDI, Social Stories, social skills groups) also exist. Psychological approaches include CBT for anxiety and depression.
- Supports (medication): Medications do not treat the core symptoms of ASD. They can help with co-occurring issues such as hyperactivity, attention, self-injury, anxiety, depression, seizures, sleep and GI problems. Work with an experienced doctor and monitor side effects.
- Supports (complementary): Special diets, supplements, chiropractic care, animal and arts therapy and similar approaches are listed as complementary and alternative. The page advises talking with a doctor before starting any of them. It does **not** call any treatment "unproven."

**1.2 NICHD, "What are the treatments for autism?"**
- URL: https://www.nichd.nih.gov/health/topics/autism/conditioninfo/treatments · Publisher: NICHD/NIH · Last reviewed 4/19/2021 · Status: 200 · **verified (fetched)**
- Supports: There is no single standard treatment for ASD. The best approach differs by person, and most people respond best to highly structured, specialized programs.
- Supports: Early diagnosis and intervention, during preschool or earlier, are more likely to have major positive effects on symptoms and later skills. People of all ages can benefit from well-designed interventions.
- Supports: The treatment types listed include behavioral management, CBT, early intervention, educational and school-based therapy, joint attention therapy, medication, nutritional therapy, OT, parent-mediated therapy, PT, social skills training and speech-language therapy.

**1.3 NICHD, "Behavioral Management Therapy for Autism"**
- URL: https://www.nichd.nih.gov/health/topics/autism/conditioninfo/treatments/behavioral-management · Last reviewed 4/19/2021 · Status: 200 · **verified (fetched)**
- Supports: Behavioral management therapy is often based on ABA. It describes PBS, Pivotal Response Training, Early Intensive Behavioral Intervention (individualized, for very young children, needs a large time commitment) and Discrete Trial Teaching. Non-ABA behavioral therapies may also help.

**1.4 NICHD, "Medication Treatment for Autism"**
- URL: https://www.nichd.nih.gov/health/topics/autism/conditioninfo/treatments/medication-treatment · Last reviewed 4/19/2021 · Status: 200 · **verified (fetched)**
- Supports: No medication cures ASD or treats all of its symptoms. Medication can reduce some related symptoms, such as self-injury, aggression, irritability, anxiety, hyperactivity and seizures. It works best combined with behavioral therapy.
- Supports: Risperidone is FDA-approved for irritability in children with autism aged 5 to 16, and aripiprazole is also listed as approved for irritability. The page does not give aripiprazole's age range. All medications carry risks.

**1.5 NICHD, "Parent-Mediated Therapy in Autism"**
- URL: https://www.nichd.nih.gov/health/topics/autism/conditioninfo/treatments/parent-mediated · Last reviewed 1/31/2017 · Status: 200 · **verified (fetched)**
- Supports: Professionals train parents to use therapy techniques with their own child, which gives the child consistent practice through the day. The page lists joint attention, social communication and behavioral types. Studies suggest gains in communication and interaction, and the evidence is still developing.
- Caution: this page was last reviewed in 2017, so it is old.

**1.6 NICHD, "Early Intervention for Autism"**
- URL: https://www.nichd.nih.gov/health/topics/autism/conditioninfo/treatments/early-intervention · Last reviewed 4/19/2021 · Status: 200 · **verified (fetched)**
- Supports: Every state has an early intervention program under IDEA Part C for young children with delays or disabilities. Typical services include family training, speech therapy, PT and nutrition services. Recent guidelines recommend starting integrated developmental and behavioral intervention as soon as ASD is diagnosed **or seriously suspected**.
- Wording note (interpretation): the page says "birth to age 2". Part C actually serves children until their third birthday. For California, use "birth to 36 months" (RCOC, 3.2).
- Caution: the page says some children later no longer meet ASD criteria. Do not turn this into "recover" language (ARTICLE_BRIEF section 4).

**1.7 NIMH, "Autism Spectrum Disorder" brochure** (replaces the NIMH topic page for treatment content)
- URL: https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/autism-spectrum-disorder · NIH Pub. No. 25-MH-8084, revised 2025 · Status: 200 · **verified (fetched)**
- Supports: Providers may prescribe medication for specific symptoms, not autism itself. Examples are aggression, anxiety, depression, attention, hyperactivity, irritability, repetitive and self-injurious behavior.
- Supports: Children may be referred for behavioral, psychological, educational, occupational, physical or speech-language therapy. Programs are often structured and intensive and may involve family members.
- Supports: Interventions work best when they begin as soon as possible after diagnosis. ASD can usually be reliably diagnosed by age 2. There is "no single best approach."

**1.8 NIMH ASD topic page**
- URL: https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/topics/autism-spectrum-disorders-asd · Last reviewed December 2024 · Status: 200 · **verified (fetched)**
- Supports only the general point that early access to services and supports can promote long-term health and well-being. It has no treatment details. Prefer 1.7.

**1.9 ASHA Practice Portal, "Autism and Autism Spectrum Disorder"** (speech-language therapy)
- URL: https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/clinical-topics/autism/ · Publisher: ASHA · No date shown · Status: 200 · **verified (fetched)**. I read the first 100k of 183k characters. The page is aimed at professionals.
- Supports: SLPs screen and assess language and social communication, check whether AAC would help, set social-language goals, train children and families to use AAC, counsel families, and take part in IEP and 504 teams.
- Supports: Care is family-centered and neurodiversity-affirming, built around the person's goals, strengths and identity.

**1.10 "Augmentative and Alternative Communication (AAC)"** (the target of ASHA's AAC redirect)
- URL: https://helpingyoucommunicate.org/article/augmentative-and-alternative-communication-aac · Publisher: Communication Health Support Association (CHSA), which says it is "powered by the expertise of" ASHA · Last reviewed May 8, 2026 · Status: 200 · **verified (fetched)**
- Supports: AAC ranges from no-tech (gestures, pictures) to speech-generating devices and apps. People of all ages can use it, including children under 3. It has **no prerequisites**: no milestones or test scores are required first. An SLP helps choose the right system.
- Note: the publisher is CHSA, not ASHA itself. Cite it as CHSA/ASHA-affiliated.

**1.11 AOTA, "Occupational Therapy Practice Guidelines for Individuals With Autism Spectrum Disorder"** (occupational therapy)
- URL: https://library.aota.org/OT_Practice_Guidelines_Autism/ · Tomchek & Koenig, AOTA Press, 2016 · Status: 200, but **paywalled**. Only the abstract and table of contents were readable. **verified (fetched), abstract only**
- Supports (abstract level only): OT supports participation in daily occupations, including self-care, education, play, leisure, social participation, and rest and sleep. Sensory integration and sensory-based approaches and family participation in daily routines are major themes.
- Caution: the source is from 2016, and I did not read the full text. For a general OT definition, the CDC (1.1) also supports "OT builds daily living skills and can include sensory integration."

**1.12 Schreibman et al. 2015, "Naturalistic Developmental Behavioral Interventions: Empirically Validated Treatments for ASD"** (developmental/naturalistic approaches)
- URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4513196/ · J Autism Dev Disord 45(8):2411–2428, DOI 10.1007/s10803-015-2407-8 · Status: 200 · **verified (fetched)**. I read the first 100k of 185k characters.
- Supports: NDBIs combine ABA with developmental science. Teaching happens in natural settings and play, with shared control, the child's own interests and natural rewards. Targets include joint attention, imitation, play and early language.
- Supports: Examples named are PRT, ESDM, JASPER, Project ImPACT, Enhanced Milieu Teaching, Reciprocal Imitation Training, Incidental Teaching, SCERTS and Early Achievements. The authors treat NDBIs as a form of ABA and note that no large RCT has compared DTT with NDBI head to head.
- Note: this is a peer-reviewed consensus paper, not a government source.

**1.13 CDC, "Early Intervention" (Learn the Signs. Act Early.)**: also see Topic 3
- URL: https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/early-intervention/index.html · Published 9/4/2025, updated 2/16/2026, reviewed 2/17/2026 · Status: 200 · **verified (fetched)**
- Supports: Early intervention serves children from birth to 3 through state programs. It is free or low-cost, eligibility is based on an evaluation, and "a doctor's referral is not necessary." Children 3 and older are evaluated through the local public school.

**1.14 FDA, "Medication Health Fraud for Specific Diseases and Conditions"** (treatments to avoid)
- URL: https://www.fda.gov/drugs/medication-health-fraud/medication-health-fraud-specific-diseases-and-conditions · Content current as of 01/28/2026 · Status: 200 · **verified (fetched)**
- Supports: The FDA calls unproven drug products sold to cure or treat autism "misleading and deceptive." They can cause serious health problems and offer false hope.
- Wording caution: the page says autism has "no cure." The article should frame this in neurodiversity-aware terms (no product can "cure" autism, and none is needed), not as a disease framing.

**1.15 FDA, "Questions and Answers on Unapproved Chelation Products"** (treatments to avoid)
- URL: https://www.fda.gov/drugs/medication-health-fraud/questions-and-answers-unapproved-chelation-products · Content current as of 02/02/2016 · Status: 200 · **verified (fetched)**
- Supports: OTC chelation products are marketed to people with autism, but the FDA has never approved any chelation product for OTC use. The FDA treats them as unapproved drugs. Chelation can cause dehydration, kidney failure and death.
- Caution: this page dates from 2016.

**1.16 RCOC Intake** (California "autism services" entry point): also see Topic 3
- URL: https://www.rcocdd.com/client-services/intake/ · © 2026 · Status: 200 · **verified (fetched)**
- Supports: RCOC serves people through Early Start (birth to 36 months), Provisional Lanterman (ages 3 to 4) and Lanterman (3 and older). A diagnosis alone does not make someone eligible. Intake and Assessment Unit: (714) 796-5354. Main line: 714-796-5100.

**2.1 BACB, BCBA page**
- URL: https://www.bacb.com/bcba/ · BCBA Handbook listed as updated June 29, 2026 · Status: 200 · **verified (fetched)**
- Supports: The BCBA is a graduate-level certification. BCBAs practice independently and supervise RBTs, BCaBAs and others who carry out behavior-analytic interventions. The steps are the handbook, eligibility, application, the Pearson VUE exam, and maintenance and renewal. The page does not list the degree, coursework or fieldwork details; those are in the handbook.

**2.2 BACB, BCaBA page**
- URL: https://www.bacb.com/bcaba/ · BCaBA Handbook updated June 29, 2026 · Status: 200 · **verified (fetched)**
- Supports: The BCaBA is an undergraduate-level certification. BCaBAs may only practice under BCBA supervision and may supervise RBTs.

**2.3 BACB, RBT page**
- URL: https://www.bacb.com/rbt/ · RBT Handbook updated June 29, 2026 · Status: 200 · **verified (fetched)**
- Supports: The RBT is a paraprofessional certification. RBTs help deliver behavior-analytic services under the direction and close supervision of an RBT Supervisor and/or RBT Requirements Coordinator. The steps are the handbook, eligibility, application, the Pearson VUE exam, and maintenance and renewal.
- Not on the page: age, education, 40-hour training, competency assessment, background check. These are in the handbook PDF (see below).

**2.4 BACB, "When to Establish an RBT Requirements Coordinator Structure within an Organization"** (RBT supervision)
- URL: https://www.bacb.com/when-to-establish-an-rbt-requirements-coordinator-structure-within-an-organization/ · Posted 11/5/2024, updated 6/29/2026 · Status: 200 · **verified (fetched)**
- Supports: The BACB has an ongoing supervision "5% minimum requirement" for RBTs. Supervision counts only when the supervisor has the RBT listed as a supervisee in their BACB account. BCBAs and qualified BCaBAs can be RBT Supervisors. The RBT Requirements Coordinator must be a BCBA and makes sure monthly requirements are met.
- Interpretation: the page confirms the 5% figure but **does not define what it is 5% of**. The search snippets said "of the hours spent providing behavior-analytic services each calendar month, with 2 face-to-face contacts, 1 of them individual." That wording comes from the RBT Handbook PDF, which I could not read (see below). Safe wording: "BACB rules require ongoing supervision of RBTs by a BCBA or BCaBA, with a 5% monthly minimum," linked to 2.4.

**2.5 BACB, "Verify BACB Certification"** (how parents verify a credential)
- URL: https://www.bacb.com/verify-certification/ · © 2026 · Status: 200 · **verified (fetched)**
- Supports: The public can use the BACB Certificant Registry, which is updated daily, to check a person's certification status and any reportable disciplinary actions. Statuses include active, inactive and expired. A $25 verification letter is also available.

**2.6 BACB, About Behavior Analysis**
- URL: https://www.bacb.com/about-behavior-analysis/ · © 2026 · Status: 200 · **verified (fetched)**

## Topic 3: EXPAND /blog/what-age-to-start-aba-therapy/ ("ABA therapy for toddlers")

### Verified sources

**3.1 CDC, "Signs and Symptoms of Autism Spectrum Disorder"**
- URL: https://www.cdc.gov/autism/signs-symptoms/index.html · Date shown May 16, 2024 · Status: 200 · **verified (fetched)**
- Supports: The page gives social-communication examples by age: 9 months (no response to name), 12 months (few gestures such as waving), 15 months (no sharing of interests), 18 months (no pointing to show), 24 months (does not notice others' distress) and 36 months (does not join other children in play). It also lists restricted and repetitive behaviors such as lining up toys, echolalia, upset at small changes, flapping or rocking, and unusual sensory reactions.
- Supports: Some children without ASD show these signs, and not every autistic child shows all of them. Talk to your child's doctor if you have concerns.

**3.2 RCOC, "Early Start: Birth to 36 Months"**
- URL: https://www.rcocdd.com/client-services/intake/early-start/ · © 2026 · Status: 200 · **verified (fetched)**
- Supports: The program serves Orange County infants and toddlers from birth to 36 months. Eligibility is one of: a delay of at least 25% in one or more developmental areas, an established risk condition, or high risk from combined biomedical factors.
- Supports: Families can apply online or through an Intake Specialist. FSN offers free developmental screenings for ages 0–5. Information packets are available in English, Spanish, Vietnamese and Korean.
- Not on the page: the 45-day timeline, the IFSP, or a list of specific therapies.

**3.3 CDC, "Early Intervention"**: see 1.13 (birth to 3, free or low-cost, no doctor's referral needed, contact the state program).

**3.4 CDC, "Learn the Signs. Act Early."**
- URL: https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/index.html · Most recent update listed Feb 17, 2026 · Status: 200 · **verified (fetched)**
- Supports: Track milestones from birth to 5 and act early if you have a concern. The free Milestone Tracker app covers 2 months to 5 years.

**3.5 CDC treatment page (ESDM for 12–48 months)**: see 1.1. Supports: ESDM applies ABA principles to children aged 12 to 48 months using play and shared attention.

**3.6 Schreibman et al. 2015 (NDBI)**: see 1.12. Supports how ABA is adapted for toddlers: play-based teaching in daily routines, following the child's lead, natural rewards, foundational targets (joint attention, imitation, play), and parent coaching during routines such as meals and bath time. The authors link parent coaching to better progress.

**3.7 NICHD Early Intervention**: see 1.6. Supports: start integrated developmental and behavioral intervention as soon as ASD is diagnosed or seriously suspected. Early intervention occurs at or before preschool age.

**3.8 NIMH brochure**: see 1.7. Supports: ASD can usually be reliably diagnosed by age 2, and interventions work best when started as soon as possible after diagnosis.

**3.9 NICHD Parent-Mediated Therapy**: see 1.5. Supports: some of these approaches can be used with children at risk of autism who are too young to be diagnosed.
