How to Choose an ABA Provider: A Parent's Checklist of Questions

By United Autism Therapy Clinical Team Published July 8, 2026 Updated September 30, 2026 7 min read

To choose an ABA provider, check that a Board Certified Behavior Analyst (BCBA) designs and supervises your child’s program, ask how goals are chosen and measured, and look for a team that respects your child’s comfort and involves you as a partner. Compare a few providers using the same questions, and watch for red flags like promised outcomes or no room for parent input.

Key takeaways

  • Verify the supervising BCBA’s certification on the BACB’s public registry.
  • Ask how goals are chosen, how progress is measured, and how often data is shared with you.
  • Look for care that respects your child’s assent, comfort, and dignity.
  • Expect to be involved through regular check-ins and parent training.
  • Be cautious of any provider that promises specific results or discourages questions.

Before you start: what you need on hand

It helps to gather a few things before you call providers:

  • Your child’s diagnosis report, if you have one.
  • Your insurance card and plan information.
  • A short list of what you most want help with, such as communication, daily routines, or play with other kids.
  • Any recent reports from school, speech therapy, occupational therapy, or your pediatrician.

If your child has Medi-Cal, ABA is covered as part of Behavioral Health Treatment for members under 21 when it is medically necessary. Our guide on whether Medi-Cal covers ABA therapy explains how that works.

Questions to ask ABA providers: the checklist

Print this section or keep it open on your phone during calls. You do not need to ask every question. Pick the ones that matter most to your family.

Credentials and supervision

A BCBA holds a graduate-level certification and can supervise others who carry out behavior-analytic services. Many sessions are run by a Registered Behavior Technician (RBT), a paraprofessional who works under close supervision.

  • Who will design my child’s program? Can I have the BCBA’s full name?
  • Are the therapists working directly with my child RBT-certified?
  • How often does the BCBA observe my child’s sessions in person?
  • How often will I meet with the BCBA?
  • What training do therapists get before working with children?
  • How do you handle staff changes, and how will you prepare my child for a new therapist?

How to verify: The BACB has a free certificant registry where you can search for a BCBA or RBT and confirm their certification type and status. It takes a minute and gives you peace of mind.

Assessment and goals

  • What assessment will you use to understand my child’s strengths and needs?
  • How will my input shape the goals?
  • Can you give me examples of goals you might set for a child like mine?
  • How do you decide whether a goal is meaningful for my child’s daily life?
  • How do you measure progress, and how often will you share data with me?
  • How often is the treatment plan reviewed and updated?
  • How do you decide how many hours my child needs?

Good answers here are specific. You want to hear about skills that matter in your child’s life, such as asking for help, playing with a sibling, getting through a doctor visit, or using a communication device. For background on how goals turn into teaching, see how ABA therapy works.

Your child’s dignity and assent

Assent means your child’s signs of willingness to take part, whether spoken or shown through actions. The BACB’s ethics code for behavior analysts includes treating others with compassion, dignity, and respect, and it asks behavior analysts to prioritize positive reinforcement.

  • How do you notice when my child is uncomfortable or does not want to participate?
  • What do therapists do when my child says “no” or pulls away?
  • How do you approach stimming and other self-regulating behaviors?
  • Do you use play and my child’s interests in teaching?
  • What is your approach to challenging behavior?
  • Do you ever use restrictive or punishment-based procedures? If so, when, and how would I be involved in that decision?

Listen for answers that focus on understanding your child, teaching new skills, and making changes when your child shows distress. The ethics code states that restrictive or punishment-based procedures should come only after less intrusive approaches have been tried. If you want to understand why these questions matter, our article on why ABA therapy can be controversial covers the history and how the field has changed.

Parent involvement

  • Is parent training part of the program? How often?
  • Can I observe sessions?
  • How will you communicate with me between sessions?
  • What will you ask me to practice at home?
  • How can I share concerns, and who do I contact?

The BACB ethics code asks behavior analysts to involve clients and relevant stakeholders, including families, in selecting goals and designing interventions. You should feel like part of the team.

Insurance and authorization

  • Do you work with my insurance plan?
  • Will you help with the authorization process?
  • What documents do you need from me?
  • What happens if my authorization runs out or needs renewal?
  • Are there any costs I should expect that insurance may not cover?

Coverage details vary by plan. Many plans, including Medi-Cal, cover ABA therapy for children with an autism diagnosis. A provider that helps you understand coverage up front can save you a lot of stress later. Our insurance page has more.

Logistics

  • Which settings do you offer: center, home, school, or a mix?
  • Which days and times are available?
  • Is there a wait before services can start?
  • What is your cancellation and make-up policy?
  • Do you offer related services, such as speech or occupational therapy?

If you are unsure which setting to choose, read our comparison of center-based vs. in-home ABA therapy.

What to look for in an ABA center or program: green flags

These signs suggest a provider that takes quality and respect seriously.

  • A BCBA who knows your child. The supervising BCBA observes sessions regularly and can talk about your child in specific terms.
  • Individual goals. Goals are written for your child, not copied from a generic list.
  • Open data. The team shows you progress data and explains what it means.
  • Play and interests. Sessions use activities your child enjoys, and learning feels engaging.
  • Respect for communication. Your child’s ways of communicating, including gestures, signs, pictures, or AAC, are honored.
  • Attention to comfort. Therapists respond to signs of distress and adjust rather than pushing through.
  • Real partnership. You are invited to observe, ask questions, and help shape goals.
  • Honest expectations. The team explains that progress varies and does not promise outcomes.
  • Coordination. The provider is willing to work with your child’s pediatrician, school, and other therapists.

Red flags when choosing an ABA provider

Be cautious if you notice any of the following.

  • Promises or guarantees. No provider can promise specific results. Be wary of anyone who says your child will “catch up” by a certain date or talks about outcomes as certain.
  • No parent involvement. You are told you cannot observe, or parent training is not offered.
  • No data sharing. The team will not show you how progress is measured, or updates are vague.
  • Punishment-focused methods. The approach relies on taking things away, scolding, or physical force instead of teaching new skills.
  • Compliance as the main goal. Goals focus mainly on making your child sit still, stop stimming, or look “less autistic,” rather than on skills that help your child.
  • Ignoring distress. Therapists push through crying or clear signs of fear without changing course.
  • Vague credentials. The provider cannot tell you who the supervising BCBA is or how often they see your child.
  • Pressure to sign quickly. You feel rushed or pushed to commit before your questions are answered.

One red flag does not always mean a provider is wrong for you. It does mean you should ask more questions and trust what you see.

Questions a good provider will ask you

A consultation should go both ways. The way a provider asks about your child tells you a lot about how they will treat your family. A thoughtful team will likely want to know:

  • What are your child’s strengths and favorite things?
  • What does a typical day look like at home?
  • What are the hardest parts of the day for your child, and for you?
  • How does your child communicate, including words, gestures, signs, pictures, or a device?
  • What would make the biggest difference in your family’s daily life right now?
  • What other services, such as speech therapy or school supports, is your child receiving?
  • Are there cultural, family, or scheduling needs the team should plan around?

If a provider asks mostly about insurance and hours, and very little about your child as a person, take note. Paperwork matters, but the best programs start with curiosity about who your child is.

How to compare your options

After your calls or visits, take a few minutes to compare providers side by side. A simple way is to rate each one from 1 to 5 on:

  1. How well they listened to you.
  2. How clearly they explained their approach.
  3. How they talked about your child’s comfort and dignity.
  4. How involved you would be.
  5. Insurance fit and practical logistics.

Numbers are just a tool. Your gut feeling about how a team treats your child matters too. If possible, watch the team interact with your child before you decide.

As you choose, it also helps to talk with your pediatrician or a qualified professional about your child’s specific needs.

Frequently asked questions

How do I check whether a BCBA is really certified?

The Behavior Analyst Certification Board runs a public certificant registry where you can search by name and see certification type and status. You can also ask the provider for the supervising BCBA's full name so you can look them up yourself. A trustworthy provider will not mind.

How many providers should I talk to before choosing?

There is no set number, but talking with two or three providers helps you compare how they answer the same questions. Pay attention to how well they listen to you, not just to what they offer. Availability and insurance fit also matter.

Is it okay to change ABA providers if things are not working?

Yes. If your concerns are not being addressed, you can look for a different provider. Ask your current team for copies of assessments and progress reports so the new team does not have to start from scratch. Check with your insurance plan about how a change affects authorization.

Should the provider be the same people who diagnosed my child?

No, and often it is not. An autism diagnosis usually comes from a professional such as a developmental pediatrician or child psychologist. ABA providers then assess your child's skills and needs to build a treatment plan.
