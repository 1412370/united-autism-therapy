Why Is ABA Therapy Controversial? History, Criticism, and Change

By United Autism Therapy Clinical Team Published July 22, 2026 Updated September 30, 2026 7 min read

ABA therapy is controversial because of its history and because many autistic people say some ABA programs taught them to hide who they are. Early programs sometimes used punishment, and critics argue that some ABA still focuses too much on compliance and on reducing harmless autistic traits like stimming. The field has changed a great deal, but those concerns are real, and families deserve an honest picture.

Key takeaways

  • Some early behavioral programs for autistic children used aversive, punishment-based methods that are widely rejected today.
  • Autistic self-advocates have raised concerns about masking, compliance-focused goals, and suppressing stimming.
  • The BACB’s current ethics code centers compassion, dignity, respect, and positive reinforcement.
  • Quality varies between providers, so the questions you ask matter.
  • Your child’s comfort, communication, and assent should guide their care.

Why this conversation matters for parents

If you have searched for ABA online, you have probably seen strong opinions on both sides. Some parents describe real progress in communication and daily life. Some autistic adults describe ABA as harmful and say it left lasting scars.

Both of these experiences exist. As a provider of ABA, we think families are best served by understanding the criticism clearly rather than having it brushed aside. Knowing where the concerns come from will help you recognize respectful care and ask better questions. If you are new to ABA, start with our overview of what ABA therapy is.

The history behind the ABA controversy

Applied behavior analysis grew out of behavioral psychology in the mid-1900s. Some of the earliest intensive programs for autistic children, in the 1960s and 1970s, used aversive procedures. These included physical punishment and, in some cases, electric shock, used to reduce behaviors such as hand-flapping, rocking, and outbursts.

Some early programs also aimed to make autistic children look as much like their non-autistic peers as possible. That framing treated autistic traits themselves as the problem.

Aversive methods are widely rejected now, but that history still shapes how many autistic people view ABA. The Autistic Self Advocacy Network (ASAN) describes aversives as “the application of pain as a means of behavior modification” and calls for them to be banned, along with limits on non-emergency restraint and seclusion, in its position statements.

What autistic self-advocates criticize about ABA

Much of today’s criticism comes from autistic people themselves, including adults who received ABA as children. ASAN, an autistic-led national nonprofit whose motto is “Nothing About Us Without Us,” is one of the best-known voices. Here are the main concerns, stated as fairly as we can.

Masking and pressure to appear non-autistic

Masking means hiding or suppressing autistic traits to fit in, such as forcing eye contact, copying others’ social behavior, or holding back movements that feel natural. Many autistic adults describe masking as exhausting and connect it to anxiety and burnout.

Critics argue that some ABA programs have taught masking directly, by setting goals like “maintains eye contact” or “sits with quiet hands.” ASAN states that it opposes behavioral programs “that focus on normalization rather than teaching useful skills.”

A focus on compliance

Another concern is compliance-heavy teaching, where the main goal is for a child to follow adult instructions quickly and without question. Critics point out that this can teach children that their “no” does not matter.

That is a real safety issue. Children, and autistic children especially, need to learn that they can refuse, set boundaries, and speak up when something feels wrong.

Suppressing stimming

Stimming, such as hand-flapping, rocking, humming, or spinning objects, often helps autistic people regulate, focus, or express joy. Programs that try to reduce harmless stimming can take away a useful coping tool. Many self-advocates see this as trying to change who a child is rather than supporting them.

Intensity and loss of childhood

Some critics raise concerns about very high weekly hours for young children and about sessions that leave little room for free play, rest, and family time.

Who sets the goals

Underneath many of these criticisms is one question: whose goals are these? Goals chosen to make a child easier to manage, or to look less autistic, are very different from goals that help a child communicate, feel safe, and take part in the life they want.

How the field of ABA has changed

Many behavior analysts have listened to these criticisms, and the field’s standards have shifted. Change is uneven, though. Not every provider practices the same way, which is why the next section matters.

A current ethics code centered on dignity

The Behavior Analyst Certification Board’s Ethics Code for Behavior Analysts took effect on January 1, 2022. Its core principles include benefiting others and treating others “with compassion, dignity, and respect.”

The code asks behavior analysts to prioritize positive reinforcement. It says restrictive or punishment-based procedures should be used only after less intrusive approaches have not worked. It also asks behavior analysts to involve clients and relevant stakeholders in selecting goals and designing interventions. You can read the full code on the BACB ethics page.

Attention to assent

Assent means a child’s signs of willingness to take part, shown through words, gestures, body language, or moving toward or away from an activity. The BACB code defines assent in its glossary.

In assent-based practice, therapists watch for these signals and respond to them. If a child turns away, cries, or says “no,” the therapist pauses, adjusts the activity, offers a break, or rebuilds trust, instead of pushing through.

Naturalistic, play-based teaching

Many programs now teach through play and daily routines rather than long stretches of drills at a table. The CDC notes that Pivotal Response Training takes place in natural settings and focuses on “pivotal” skills that help a child learn many others. Naturalistic approaches follow the child’s interests, which tends to make learning more enjoyable.

Meaningful goals

Many providers today focus on goals that improve the child’s own quality of life, such as:

  • Communicating wants, needs, and “no” in whatever way works for the child, including AAC.
  • Asking for a break or for help.
  • Coping with hard moments, like a doctor visit or a haircut.
  • Daily living skills that build independence.
  • Playing and connecting with others in ways the child enjoys.
  • Reducing behaviors that cause real harm, such as self-injury, by teaching safer ways to meet the same need.

Family collaboration

Families are now more often treated as partners who help choose goals, not just as people who carry out instructions at home. Parent training and regular check-ins are common parts of quality programs.

Room for more change

Many autistic advocates say these changes do not go far enough, and some believe ABA cannot be reformed. Those views are part of an ongoing conversation, and we think providers should keep listening to autistic people as practices continue to evolve.

What families can ask to ensure respectful ABA care

These questions can help you tell the difference between respectful care and the practices critics describe.

About goals

  • Who chooses my child’s goals, and how will my child’s preferences be considered?
  • Are any goals about eye contact, “quiet hands,” or looking less autistic? Why?
  • How does each goal help my child’s own daily life?

About stimming and autistic traits

  • How do you approach stimming?
  • Which behaviors would you leave alone, and which would you work on? Why?

About assent and comfort

  • How do therapists recognize when my child is uncomfortable or saying “no”?
  • What happens when my child refuses or becomes upset?
  • Do you teach my child how to refuse or ask for a break?

About methods

  • Do you ever use punishment or restrictive procedures? Under what conditions, and how would I be involved?
  • How much of my child’s day is play-based or built into natural routines?

About transparency

  • Can I observe sessions at any time?
  • How will you share progress data with me?

If your child is already in ABA

Reading criticism of ABA after your child has started can be unsettling. You do not need to panic or stop services overnight. Instead, use what you have learned to look more closely.

  • Review the goals. Ask for a copy of your child’s current treatment plan. For each goal, ask yourself whether it helps your child communicate, stay safe, build independence, or enjoy life.
  • Watch a session. Notice how the therapist responds when your child is upset, says no, or needs a break.
  • Ask direct questions. A good BCBA will explain the reason for each goal and change goals that do not serve your child.
  • Share your values. Tell the team what matters to your family, such as keeping stimming, respecting “no,” or protecting free play time.

If your concerns are brushed off, or you are told not to worry about how your child feels, that is useful information. You can ask for changes, request a different BCBA, or look for another provider.

Watching how your child responds

Beyond any answer a provider gives, your child’s own response is important information. Over time, notice:

  • Does your child seem comfortable going to sessions?
  • Do they seem relaxed with their therapist?
  • Are they gaining ways to communicate, including ways to say “no”?
  • Do they still have time and space to be themselves, including stimming?

If something feels wrong, trust that feeling and raise it with the BCBA. A good team will welcome it. Understanding autism as a different way of experiencing the world, not a problem to erase, is a helpful starting point. Our article on understanding autism spectrum disorder explores this further.

It is also wise to talk with your pediatrician or a qualified professional about your child’s specific needs and the full range of support options.

Frequently asked questions

Do all autistic adults oppose ABA?

No. Autistic people hold a range of views about ABA, from strong opposition to support for certain respectful approaches. Many autistic self-advocates have raised serious concerns, and those concerns deserve to be taken seriously by families and providers alike.

What does neurodiversity-affirming ABA mean?

The term is used in different ways, and there is no single official definition. Generally, it describes care that accepts autism as part of who a child is, avoids targeting harmless autistic traits, pays close attention to assent, and focuses on goals that improve the child's own quality of life.

Should ABA try to stop my child from stimming?

Stimming often helps a child regulate, focus, or express feelings, and harmless stimming usually does not need to be reduced. If a behavior causes injury or seriously gets in the way of learning or safety, a BCBA can help find safer options that still meet your child's needs, with your input.

Is it okay to have mixed feelings about ABA?

Yes. Many parents feel torn after reading criticism online. Asking hard questions, observing sessions, and watching how your child responds are all reasonable ways to decide what is right for your family.
