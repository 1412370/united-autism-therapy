Preparing Your Home for In-Home ABA Therapy: A Practical Checklist

By United Autism Therapy Clinical Team Published September 18, 2026 Updated October 5, 2026 7 min read

Quick answer

Preparing your home for in-home ABA usually means choosing a small, consistent session space, doing a quick safety check, setting aside a few favorite toys and snacks, planning a regular session time, and making sure an adult will be home. You do not need special equipment or a perfect house. Your child's therapy team will help you adjust the setup as they learn your child.

Preparing your home for in-home ABA is simpler than most parents expect. You need a small, fairly calm space, a quick safety check, a few of your child’s favorite toys and snacks, a regular session time, and an adult at home. You do not need a playroom or special equipment. Your child’s therapy team will help fine-tune the setup once they get to know your child.

Key takeaways

  • A corner of a room or a spot at the kitchen table is often enough for sessions.
  • A quick, child’s-eye-level safety check helps sessions run smoothly.
  • Setting aside a few favorite items keeps them motivating during therapy time.
  • A steady schedule, planned around naps and meals, helps your child know what to expect.
  • The first sessions usually focus on building trust through play.

Where should in-home ABA sessions take place?

Pick one main spot and use it most of the time. Consistency helps your child learn that this space is where sessions happen.

A good session space usually has:

  • A small table and chairs that fit your child, or a clear spot on the floor
  • Some open floor space for movement and play
  • A little distance from the TV, gaming systems, and busy walkways
  • Decent lighting and a comfortable temperature
  • A nearby shelf or bin where the therapist can keep materials out of reach

The living room, a corner of your child’s bedroom, a dining table, or a quiet part of the kitchen can all work. Some goals will also take place in other rooms. Handwashing happens at the sink, and dressing happens where your child gets dressed. The therapist may move around the house depending on the goal.

If your child strongly associates one room with free time or rest, like their bed, a different spot may work better for focused learning. Your child’s BCBA can help you decide.

How do I make my home safe for ABA sessions?

A quick safety check is good practice for any home with young children. It also lets the therapist focus on teaching instead of hazards.

The American Academy of Pediatrics suggests getting down to your child’s eye level to spot dangers you might miss. Its home safety tips include:

  • Lock cabinets that hold cleaning products and other dangerous items.
  • Keep all medicines out of reach, including those in a purse or bag.
  • Put safety plugs in unused outlets and keep cords out of reach.
  • Check floors often for small objects like coins, buttons, and batteries.

The AAP also recommends anchoring furniture and TVs with anti-tip devices and avoiding placing toys or remotes up high, where they can tempt a child to climb.

For sessions specifically, it also helps to:

  • Know where exits are and whether doors or gates need to stay closed.
  • Tell the therapist if your child tends to run off, climb, or mouth objects.
  • Share any allergies, including food allergies, before snacks are used in sessions.
  • Keep pets in another room if your child or the therapist finds them distracting.

What materials and toys should I have ready?

You do not need to buy much. Many providers bring some teaching materials. Ask what your team brings before you go shopping.

Helpful things to have on hand:

  • A few of your child’s favorite toys, set aside so they stay special
  • Small, easy snacks your child enjoys, if snacks are part of the plan
  • Everyday items for routine goals, like your child’s cup, toothbrush, and clothes
  • Labeled bins or baskets for toys, with a picture on each one for cleanup practice
  • A spot on the wall or fridge for a visual schedule
  • Your child’s communication tools, such as picture cards or an AAC device

Why set favorites aside? Toys your child can play with all day may lose their pull. Keeping a few out of sight between sessions can make them more motivating during therapy. This connects to reinforcement, which means responding in a way that makes a behavior more likely to happen again. The BACB notes that ABA techniques generally teach new skills through positive reinforcement.

A visual schedule is a row of pictures that shows what will happen and in what order. Many therapists set one up on day one. Leave it in place between sessions if the therapist suggests it.

How should I plan our family schedule around sessions?

Predictable timing helps many children settle in. The NICHD notes that many people with autism respond best to highly structured programs, and a steady session time is part of that structure.

When choosing session times, think about:

  • When your child is usually most alert and rested
  • Nap times, meal times, and bedtime
  • School, daycare, or other therapy appointments
  • Siblings’ schedules, such as school pickup
  • Times when an adult can reliably be home

Most in-home programs require a parent or another responsible adult to be home during sessions. Ask your provider about their policy and plan for it in advance.

On session days, try to:

  • Have your child fed, changed, or toileted shortly before the session starts.
  • Turn off the TV and put away screens in the session area.
  • Let siblings know what to expect and where they can play.
  • Keep visitors and big errands for another time when you can.

If something comes up, like an illness or a schedule change, let your team know as early as you can.

What should I expect in the first few weeks?

The first sessions often look a lot like play. This is on purpose. The therapist is building trust through pairing, which means becoming linked with fun and good things before asking much of your child.

In the early weeks, you may see:

  • The therapist following your child’s lead and joining favorite activities
  • Short, easy requests mixed into play
  • The BCBA observing, asking questions, and learning your routines
  • A visual schedule or first-then card being introduced

Your child may test the new routine, protest, or need time to warm up. That is common. Progress varies from child to child, and early sessions are not a sign of how things will always go.

Your role matters too. Depending on the plan, you may watch, take part, or practice strategies with the therapist’s support. Registered Behavior Technicians (RBTs), who often lead sessions, are certified paraprofessionals who work under the direction and close supervision of a qualified supervisor. Ask how and when the supervising BCBA will check in with you.

What should I share with the therapy team before day one?

The more the team knows, the faster they can connect with your child.

  • Favorite toys, shows, songs, snacks, and activities
  • Things your child dislikes or finds upsetting, like loud sounds or certain textures
  • How your child communicates, including words, signs, pictures, or a device
  • Typical daily schedule and the routines that are hardest right now
  • Allergies and any safety concerns
  • House rules, like shoes off, pets, or rooms that are off limits
  • Cultural or family preferences you want the team to respect

If sounds, lights, or textures affect your child strongly, our guide to sensory processing and autism may help you think about the space.

Printable checklist for in-home ABA

Space

  • One main session spot chosen
  • Small table and chair, or clear floor space
  • TV and screens off or out of view

Safety

  • Cleaning products and medicines locked or out of reach
  • Furniture and TV anchored
  • Outlets covered and cords tucked away
  • Floors checked for small objects
  • Allergies and safety concerns shared with the team

Materials

  • A few favorite toys set aside
  • Preferred snacks on hand, if part of the plan
  • Labeled bins for toys
  • Wall or fridge spot for a visual schedule
  • Communication tools within reach

Schedule

  • Regular session times chosen around naps and meals
  • An adult available to be home during sessions
  • Siblings and other caregivers know what to expect

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to buy special materials for in-home ABA?

Usually not. Many sessions use your child's own toys, snacks, and household items, and therapists often bring some teaching materials. Ask your provider what they bring and whether anything would help before you buy something new.

What if we live in a small apartment?

A small space can work well. A corner of the living room or a spot at the kitchen table is often enough. What matters most is that the space is fairly calm and is used in a similar way each time.

Can my child's siblings be home during sessions?

Yes, siblings are usually welcome to be home. Some sessions may include them, and others may go better with fewer distractions. Talk with the therapist about when siblings can join and what they can do during quieter teaching times.

What should I tell the therapist before the first visit?

Share your child's favorite toys, snacks, and activities, any known triggers, how your child communicates, and any allergies or house rules, like shoes off or pets in another room. This helps the therapist build trust quickly and keeps everyone safe.
