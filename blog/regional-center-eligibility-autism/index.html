Regional Center Eligibility for Autism in Orange County: What Lanterman Requires

By United Autism Therapy Clinical Team Published November 16, 2026 5 min read

Quick answer

For children age 3 and older, the Lanterman criteria used by the Regional Center of Orange County name autism as a qualifying developmental disability. The condition must begin before age 18, be expected to continue indefinitely, and cause significant limitations in at least three of seven life areas, including self-care, language and learning. Eligibility is decided against these criteria, not on the diagnosis alone.

Many families first hear the phrase “regional center eligibility” after a hard conversation with a doctor or school. It can sound like a test your child has to pass. In practice, it is a set of written criteria that the Regional Center of Orange County (RCOC) applies when it decides whether a person can receive regional center services. This article explains how those criteria work for children age 3 and older, so you know what the intake team is looking at before you call.

Key takeaways

  • For age 3 and older, RCOC uses the Lanterman criteria, and autism is named as a qualifying developmental disability.
  • The condition must start before age 18 and be expected to continue indefinitely.
  • The person must have significant limitations in at least three of seven life areas.
  • A condition that is solely psychiatric, solely a learning disability, or solely physical does not qualify.
  • Eligibility is decided against the criteria, so a diagnosis alone is not the whole picture.

What the Lanterman criteria are

The Lanterman criteria are the eligibility rules for regional center services for people age 3 and older. RCOC’s Lanterman page says the criteria are defined by law, specifically in section 4512 of the California Welfare and Institutions Code. You do not need to memorize the statute to use it. What matters is the three parts that the intake team applies: the qualifying disability, its timing and duration, and the functional limitations it causes.

RCOC’s intake guide explains how to start the process. This article focuses on the eligibility question itself, so you can prepare for the conversation.

Part 1: Autism is a qualifying developmental disability

RCOC’s Lanterman page lists autism among the qualifying developmental disabilities. The list also includes cerebral palsy, intellectual disability and epilepsy, along with conditions closely related to intellectual disability or that need similar treatment.

Autism is named directly, which is important for families. It also means that your child’s autism diagnosis is relevant information for the intake team. Bring the diagnostic report or your child’s medical records if you have them. If your child is still being evaluated, say so when you call. The CDC’s signs and symptoms page describes the range of ways autism can appear, which can help you describe your child’s daily life clearly.

Part 2: The condition must begin early and last

The Lanterman criteria also look at timing and duration. The disability must:

  • Originate before age 18. The condition must have started during childhood, not later in adulthood.
  • Be expected to continue indefinitely. The intake team needs to see that the condition is long-term, not a short-term delay that is expected to resolve.

A diagnostic report and a developmental history usually document these two points. If the timing is unclear, describe when you first noticed differences, what you saw, and how your child’s skills have changed since then.

Part 3: Significant limitations in at least three of seven areas

This part is often the most confusing. RCOC’s criteria ask whether the person has significant limitations in at least three of seven life areas:

  1. Self-care
  2. Language
  3. Learning
  4. Mobility
  5. Self-direction
  6. Capacity for independent living
  7. Economic self-sufficiency

“Significant” is the key word. It means the limitation affects daily functioning in a meaningful way, not that a skill is simply slower than average. A child who is still building language, who needs help with self-care routines, and who has learning differences may meet this part of the criteria. Your child’s evaluation reports, therapy notes, and school records can document these limitations.

Think about specific examples for each area. For example, “does not use words to request help during dinner” or “needs hand-over-hand help with dressing” gives the intake team more to work with than “delayed.” Concrete descriptions from your daily life are valuable here.

What does not qualify

The criteria also list exclusions. A condition that is solely psychiatric, solely a learning disability, or solely physical does not qualify under this part of the Lanterman criteria. A child may have more than one condition, so the intake team looks at the full picture rather than a single label. If you are unsure how your child’s conditions fit, raise it at intake rather than assuming the answer.

Residence in Orange County

RCOC serves people who live in Orange County. If your family lives in another county, RCOC’s page directs you to the state directory to find your local regional center. Confirm your address before you start, since an address outside Orange County changes which regional center you should contact.

How the decision is made

RCOC’s Lanterman page describes the process in three steps. You request information from an Intake Specialist through the inquiry form on the RCOC website. Eligibility is then determined against the criteria above. RCOC also offers a three-part video series on determining eligibility for ages 3 and older, in English, Spanish and Vietnamese, which can help you understand the process before you call.

Keep copies of everything you send, write down the names of the people you speak with, and ask the intake specialist what documents they need. RCOC’s pages do not list required documents, so ask for that list directly.

If the intake specialist asks for records you do not have yet, tell them what you have and what you are requesting. Eligibility conversations often happen in stages, and a clear list of what is still pending helps both sides plan. Ask for the next step in writing, so you know when to follow up and what you are waiting for.

Eligibility and therapy are separate questions

Parents often ask whether they need regional center eligibility before starting therapy. Therapy and regional center services can run on different tracks. An ABA program, speech therapy, or occupational therapy can be arranged based on your child’s needs and your family’s choices, while the regional center process runs in parallel. Ask the intake specialist how their services fit with the therapy your child is already receiving.

Starting therapy does not depend on the regional center decision. Talk with your child’s pediatrician to plan evaluations and supports while the eligibility process moves forward. If your child is school-age, an IEP request is a separate process through the school district.

What to bring to the conversation

Bring a short summary of your child’s strengths and needs, grouped by the seven areas above. Include any diagnostic reports, school plans, and therapy evaluations. Write down the questions you want to ask, such as what documents are needed, what happens after intake, and how long the process typically takes. RCOC’s pages do not state timelines, so ask for the expected timing directly.

If you want help describing your child’s daily functioning in clear terms, our team can talk with you about your child’s needs. You can reach us at (714) 709-7819 or through our contact page. Talk with your child’s pediatrician about developmental concerns and specific needs, since they know your child’s history best.

Talk with our team

Whether your child is just starting the eligibility process or already receiving services, our team can help you think through the next steps. Start by listing what your child can do, what is hard, and where you most need support at home and in the community. That list is useful for the regional center, for your child’s doctor, and for any therapy team you work with.

Frequently asked questions

Does an autism diagnosis automatically qualify my child for regional center services?

Not by itself. RCOC's Lanterman page explains that eligibility is determined against the Lanterman criteria, which include a qualifying developmental disability and significant limitations in at least three of seven life areas. A diagnosis is important information, but the intake team decides eligibility using these criteria.

What are the seven life areas RCOC uses?

The Lanterman criteria list self-care, language, learning, mobility, self-direction, capacity for independent living, and economic self-sufficiency. A person must have significant limitations in at least three of these areas to meet the functional criteria.

Is autism named in the Lanterman criteria?

Yes. RCOC's Lanterman page names autism in its list of qualifying developmental disabilities, alongside conditions such as cerebral palsy, intellectual disability and epilepsy. The list also includes conditions closely related to intellectual disability or that need similar treatment.

What if my child is under 3?

RCOC uses a separate Early Start page for infants and toddlers from birth to 36 months, with its own criteria based on developmental delay or known risk conditions. Start with the Early Start page for children in that age range, and ask the intake specialist which page applies.
