Benefits of ABA Therapy for Children, and Its Limits

By United Autism Therapy Clinical Team Published July 1, 2026 Updated September 30, 2026 7 min read

The main benefits of ABA therapy are practical: many children build skills in communication, daily living, play, and safety, and find easier ways to get their needs met. Because ABA is individualized and data-driven, the team can see what is helping and change course when something is not. It also has real limitations, and knowing both sides helps you make the best choice for your child.

Key takeaways

  • Research supports ABA as one effective option for helping many autistic children build skills, though results vary.
  • Common areas of growth include communication, self-care, social play, safety, and replacing behaviors that interfere with learning.
  • Families often benefit too, through parent training and more predictable daily routines.
  • ABA takes time and commitment, and quality varies between providers.
  • The best programs respect your child’s comfort and consent, and set goals that matter to your family.

Why ABA is widely used

ABA is one of several approaches the CDC lists under behavioral treatments for autism. The CDC notes that ABA encourages desired behaviors and discourages undesired behaviors to improve a variety of skills, and that progress is tracked and measured. The Behavior Analyst Certification Board adds that ABA approaches have demonstrated effectiveness across many areas of practice.

That does not mean ABA is the answer for every child. NICHD reminds families that the most effective therapies and interventions are often different for each person. Think of the benefits below as possibilities, not promises.

Benefits of ABA therapy for children: skill areas

Here is what growth can look like in each area. These are examples, not guarantees. Your child’s goals should come from their own assessment.

Communication

For many families, this is the biggest one. ABA can help a child:

  • Ask for things they want or need, like food, a break, or help.
  • Say “no,” “stop,” or “all done” in a safe way.
  • Answer questions, make comments, or start a conversation.
  • Use signs, pictures, or a speech-generating device when spoken words are hard.

A child who can reliably say “break please” often has less reason to run from the table or throw materials. Communication and behavior are closely linked.

ABA teams often work alongside speech and language therapy. The American Speech-Language-Hearing Association notes that speech-language pathologists assess and teach communication and social skills, including alternative forms of communication.

Daily living and independence

Skills like dressing, toileting, handwashing, brushing teeth, and eating a range of foods can make a huge difference in a family’s day. ABA breaks these tasks into small, teachable steps and practices them in the routines where they happen.

For an older child, independence goals might include packing a backpack, following a visual schedule, or making a simple snack.

Social and play skills

ABA can support skills such as:

  • Taking turns and sharing materials.
  • Joining a game with a sibling or classmate.
  • Noticing when someone says their name.
  • Playing with toys in new ways, or for longer stretches.

Social goals should respect your child’s personality. The aim is to give your child tools to connect in ways that feel good to them, not to force eye contact or make them act like someone else.

Safety

Safety goals can be some of the most meaningful, especially for younger children. Examples include:

  • Staying close to a caregiver in stores and parking lots.
  • Stopping when an adult says “wait” or “stop.”
  • Coming back when called.
  • Sharing a name or showing an ID card if lost.

Reducing behaviors that interfere with learning

Some behaviors, like hitting, self-injury, or running away, can put a child at risk or make it hard to learn and join activities. ABA looks for the reason behind these behaviors, then teaches a safer way to meet the same need.

For example, if a child bangs their head when a task is too hard, the team might teach them to ask for help, make the task easier at first, and build up slowly. The goal is not simply to stop a behavior. It is to replace it with something that works better for your child.

Benefits for the whole family

ABA is not only about sessions with a therapist. Many families find that the program changes day-to-day life at home too.

  • Practical strategies. Through parent training, a BCBA shows you techniques you can use during meals, bedtime, or outings.
  • More predictable routines. Tools like visual schedules and timers can make transitions smoother for everyone.
  • A shared language. Parents, therapists, and sometimes teachers use the same approach, so your child gets consistent support.
  • Clearer understanding. Learning why a behavior happens can make hard moments feel less confusing and less personal.
  • Support for siblings. Goals like turn-taking and shared play can make sibling time easier.

Individualized and data-driven: why it matters

Two features set ABA apart and support many of its benefits.

It is individualized. Your child’s program should be built from their own assessment, strengths, and interests. A four-year-old learning to request and a twelve-year-old learning to manage a morning routine will have very different plans.

It is data-driven. Therapists record progress in every session, and the BCBA reviews that data to make decisions. If a strategy is not helping after a reasonable period, the plan changes. You can learn more about this cycle in our guide to how ABA therapy works.

What progress can look like on paper

Benefits are easier to judge when you can see them. Here is a simple, made-up example of how one goal might be tracked for a four-year-old who grabs toys from classmates.

Goal: Your child asks for a turn with a peer’s toy, using words, a sign, or a picture card, in at least 8 out of 10 chances across two weeks, with no physical help.

  • At the start: Your child reaches or grabs almost every time. The therapist models “my turn” and gently guides the request.
  • A few weeks in: Your child asks on their own some of the time, often with a quick reminder.
  • Later: Your child asks independently most of the time, with the therapist, then with a parent at home, then at preschool.

Each step is recorded, so no one has to rely on memory or a general feeling that “things seem better.” If progress stalls, the BCBA can see it and try a different approach, such as a new visual cue or more practice during play.

When you meet with your child’s BCBA, ask to see goals written this way. Clear goals make it much easier to tell whether ABA is actually helping your child.

Pros and cons of ABA therapy

A balanced view helps you set realistic expectations.

Pros Cons and considerations
Research supports ABA for building many kinds of skills Results vary, and progress is rarely a straight line
Goals are specific and measurable Programs can require many hours each week
Progress is tracked, so plans can change quickly Quality varies widely between providers
Can be delivered at a center, at home, or at school Scheduling can be demanding for families
Parents learn strategies they can use every day Insurance authorization can take time and paperwork
Can be combined with speech and occupational therapy Some autistic adults have raised concerns about past practices

The criticism is worth hearing

Some autistic adults and advocates have criticized ABA, especially older approaches that focused on making children appear less autistic, relied on long, repetitive drills, or ignored a child’s distress. Many providers have changed their practice in response, but not all programs are the same.

We look at these concerns in depth in our article on why ABA therapy is controversial.

What to watch for in any ABA program

Positive signs:

  • Your child generally seems comfortable and engaged with their therapist.
  • Goals focus on skills that help your child, like communication and independence.
  • The team uses your child’s interests, and play is part of learning.
  • Your child can say or show “no” or “break,” and the team respects it.
  • The BCBA explains goals and data clearly and welcomes your questions.

Warning signs:

  • Goals focus on stopping harmless behaviors, like hand-flapping, without a clear reason tied to your child’s well-being.
  • Your child seems consistently distressed, and the team pushes through.
  • You are discouraged from watching sessions or asking questions.
  • The BCBA rarely sees your child or reviews the program.
  • Progress is not being shared with you.

If something concerns you, raise it with the BCBA. Behavior analysts certified by the BACB must follow its ethics code, and the BACB offers information on ethics and how to report concerns. For a practical checklist, see our guide on how to choose an ABA provider.

Is ABA a good fit for your child?

ABA may be a strong fit if your child would benefit from structured support with communication, daily routines, or behaviors that are affecting safety or learning. It may also be one part of a broader plan that includes occupational therapy, speech therapy, and school services.

Every child is different. Talk with your pediatrician or a qualified professional about your child’s specific needs before deciding on any therapy.

Frequently asked questions

How soon will we see benefits from ABA?

It depends on your child, their goals, and how often they receive services. Some families notice small changes, like fewer hard transitions, fairly early, while bigger skills take longer. Your child's BCBA should review progress data with you regularly so you can see what is changing.

Can ABA help a child who does not speak?

Yes. ABA can teach communication in many forms, including signs, picture exchange, and speech-generating devices, not only spoken words. The aim is for your child to have a reliable way to share needs and ideas. Working with a speech-language pathologist can add a lot here.

Is ABA right for every autistic child?

Not necessarily. Research supports ABA for many children, but no single approach fits everyone, and some families choose other supports or a mix. Talk with your pediatrician and care team about your child's needs, and choose what fits your child and family.

What should I do if I am uncomfortable with something in my child's ABA program?

Speak with the BCBA first and ask them to explain the goal and method. A good provider will listen, explain their reasoning, and make changes if something is not right for your child. If you still have concerns, you can seek a second opinion or contact the BACB about ethics issues.
